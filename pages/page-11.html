English Translation

Writing was not permitted at all, not even transcription. This also appears from the incident concerning Rabbi Chiya bar Abba mentioned there. And since Rabbi Yochanan and Resh Lakish were studying from a book, they found it necessary to rely on the principle, "a time to act for the Lord." The intention is that since they could not acquire these matters orally, as they did with the Mishnayot, the Baraitot, and the Talmud, in order to recite them publicly or when they needed to—for Aggadot are not essential, and Halakhah is not derived from them, serving only for ethical instruction, proper conduct, and the like—therefore, since their time was insufficient to acquire them orally, the Sages permitted studying Aggadah from a book on account of "a time to act for the Lord." For, in the final analysis, it is less stringent than Halakhot, concerning which it is stated, "These—these you may write, but you may not write Halakhot," and Aggadot are not considered Halakhot. Therefore, they chose to avail themselves of a lenient prohibition, not a stringent one.
I have already observed the Gaon, Rabbi Tzaddik Chayyim, may his light shine, in his glosses published in new editions of the Talmud in Vienna. He endeavors to prove that Aggadah is not at all included in the category of Halakhot, and was permitted from ancient times. He adduced proof either from the precise wording that they said "those who write Halakhot" and did not say "those who write Aggadot," or from the incident concerning Rabbi Chiya, which is cited in the Jerusalem Talmud, chapter three of Tractate Kilayim, and in Parashat ha-Nose, and in Bereshit Rabbah, chapter thirty-three, where he was looking at a book of Aggadic interpretations of Psalms. Therefore, he rejected the words of Rashi, who included Aggadot in the prohibition against writing Oral Torah, and interpreted the plain meaning of the passage as referring to Shabbat, as may be seen by one who examines his words, may his light shine.
I cannot elaborate further, except to state that the Jerusalem Talmud in Tractate Kilayim, and in Parashat ha-Nose, only says, "I cast my eyes upon a book of Psalms, Aggadah." And if so, who claims that he had a book of Aggadah in his hand? Perhaps he had a book of Psalms in his hand, and was perusing it to compose his Aggadic interpretations? Or perhaps he had no book at all in his hand, but was merely contemplating the Oral Torah, specifically the Aggadic interpretations of Psalms. And this is what appears to me from the wording cited in Bereshit Rabbah, "I was contemplating the Aggadic interpretations of Psalms," for the term "contemplation" usually refers to intellectual contemplation. And although this interpretation is somewhat forced, nevertheless there is no conclusive proof from here that they were already writing and permitted to study from written texts.
For if so, what is the meaning of all the alarm expressed by Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi when he stated: "He who writes it has no portion in the World to Come, and he who expounds upon it is either singed or blessed"? And would Rabbi Chiya, the holy, be suspected of expounding upon it from a book? And of being among the blessed, Blessed be He! Furthermore, in all the sayings cited in Shemot Rabbah, chapter forty-seven, concerning this matter, from the beginning of the passage to its end, Aggadot are also included within the prohibition of Oral Torah which a man is not permitted to utter, and some of them even mention Aggadot before Halakhot, as may be seen there. Furthermore, his extraction of the subject of Aggadot from the plain meaning, contending that it does not refer to the prohibition of writing Oral Torah, is not acceptable to me, for Rabbi Chiya bar Abba said, "If there is any good thing in it, let the hand that wrote it be cut off," because these were matters of Oral Torah in writing, as explained by Penei Moshe there, and there is no other interpretation for these words. The sum of the matter is that the Gaon, Rabbi Tzaddik Chayyim, may his light shine, has confused us on these points.
Be that as it may, we have found further proof that writing was permitted, and the primary prohibition was only concerning study, in accordance with the opinion of the Geonim of Sepharad, and this is clear and elucidated. And let not the Sages of Israel be provoked by me, for I shall interpret four sayings that appear consecutively in the Jerusalem Talmud, in the chapter Ein Om'dim, in the manner of the aforementioned: The first states: "He who labors in his study in the Bet ha-Keneset will not quickly forget it." The second: "A covenant is established: he who labors in his study in private will not quickly forget it," as it is said, "and with the modest is wisdom." The third: "He who studies Aggadah from a book will not quickly forget it." The fourth: In the name of Rabbenu Tam, who holds that "his study will not quickly be forgotten," what is the reason? "Lest you forget the things your eyes have seen." We shall not elaborate on the difficulties and precise textual analyses over which the commentators of the Jerusalem Talmud have labored extensively, yet without producing anything truly satisfying. And the greatest difficulty among them all is that these two sayings appear contradictory. For the Bet ha-Keneset is a place where many people frequent, and even when the regular communal prayers are not being recited, it is still a public domain, and at the very least, not a private place. If so, how can "in private" which was stated thereafter, be interpreted? And to interpret it in the manner of modesty and absence of pride, as the commentators have explained, would be to deviate from the plain meaning of the words.
It is true that Maimonides appears not to have read, "He who labors in his study in the Bet ha-Keneset," but rather, "He who labors in his study," and if so, he correctly ruled concerning the one who studies in private. Be that as it may, according to our textual reading, it presents a difficulty. Furthermore, how can the verse, "Be careful lest you forget the things your eyes have seen," serve as proof for one who holds that his study will not quickly be forgotten? And Rabbi Moreh Hora'ah Rabbi Adoniram, of blessed memory, in his commentary there, explains thus: "Lest you forget the things, etc. It seems to me that this refers to both: to one who studies from a book, which is 'that which your eyes have seen'; and also the expression 'seeing of the intellect' and 'understanding of the heart' is implied for Rabbenu Tam, for speech cannot be seen with one's physical eyes, but only with the eye of the intellect." Thus far his words. And one kisses the lips of him who offers upright words, resting upon the laws and ordinances of nature. For our nature dictates that we cannot acquire an ordered sequence of matters orally, unless they were first acquired.
