הענף הב': ביאור אגרת רב שרירא גאון — Branch Two: Elucidation of Rav Sherira Gaon's Epistle
שערי תשובה סי' קפ"ז

צילום כתב היד / Scan

נוסח עברי

על ד' סעיפי הספק הב' בטוב טעם ודעת, ובראיות מופתיות על כל דבר ודבר (כי על הסעיף הה' שהוא ענין הכתיבה שמר עצמו להשיב באחרונה כי שם מקומו יען שהוא ספק נופל על כל התשבע"פ עד סוף חתי' התלמוד, וכמו שהשואל הרחיב ספקו במשנה, ובברייתות ובתלמוד לא אמר כי אם "וכן התלמוד כיצד נכתב" וכו' ודוק היטב) דרך ובא לו להתטפל להשיב אל הס' הג' על מאי דחזינן דאית

English Translation

Regarding the four sections of the second doubt, with sound reasoning and demonstrative proofs for each and every point, he proceeds to address the third section regarding what we find, namely, that the version of the *Yochasin* text is of significant import, for since it is in writing, there is an aspect of potential harm, lest they come to read from the written text. Therefore, this matter is not performed or completed except out of necessity. And on this, it was rightly concluded that they were not in need of compositions—that is to say, written texts before them—for otherwise, these are matters that are taught orally, meaning, that they are forbidden to teach them from a written text. If so, why were they in need of an organized compilation before them?
And if it was due to forgetfulness, of this there was no fear, for they were 'each man under his vine and under his fig tree,' and each one knew them through tradition; and there was no fear of forgetfulness. Therefore, they were not in need of compositions, nor of writing them, until the Holy Temple was destroyed and the troubles multiplied, and forgetfulness grew strong, and the students who did not know them in their entirety were in need of compositions. And it is a good proof from Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrkanos, who did not say anything that he did not hear from his master. And why did he not hear all that his master knew, after serving him completely, as is known? But rather, because of the confusions, the persecutions, and the destructions, his master did not have sufficient time to transmit to his student all that he knew, nor was he able to gather everything. And in this way, we lost several traditions, which we are compelled in every generation to recover through *pilpul* and methods of *hora'ah* and *hekesh*. And by this measure, the words of the Gaon, may his memory be a blessing, are weighed in the holy balance, for through a hidden written compilation, even if there are a thousand troubles in one generation, because of which teachers and students do not see one another, at any rate, it is clear that when the storm of troubles subsides, they will return to their study through the hidden compilations. But according to the version of the *Chiddushim*, what proof is there from Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrkanos?
Until now, the Gaon says that the early Sages were not in need of compiling matters that were taught orally, and now they are in need of it. For what reason did he not say anything? And it is not proper to state the reason of forgetfulness, and it remained unaddressed in the Gaon's pen, for the matter at hand is based on oral tradition, and still without any mention of writing at all. And what good is it, and what proof is there from Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrkanos, who did not say anything that he did not hear from his master? For the intention is certainly that he lacked some of his master's knowledge due to the confusions. And what correction is there for this through an oral tradition and a single mode of expression, without any writing? And indeed, even his distinguished master, Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai—it is certain that all his traditions were oral and of a single expression, and yet his chief students did not know some of them due to the persecutions. And it already appears that the author of the [variant] version felt himself that after he omitted every mention of writing and compilation from the letter and inverted the matters as mentioned above, his eye saw that the proof of Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrkanos had no connection whatsoever with what preceded it. And for this reason, instead of 'and they were in need of compilations,' which is written in *Yochasin*, he altered it and wrote 'and they would say [teachings] in their name.' That is to say, besides the benefit gained by omitting 'in need of compilations,' which implies a written compilation, furthermore, through this the proof of Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrkanos truly becomes substantive, for he did not say anything he did not hear from his master, from which it is inferred that they would say [teachings] in their name. But this is vain and false, for even this, besides not being any proof at all that they would say [teachings] in their name—the primary intention there is that even if he knew how to answer by his own reasoning, he was fearful of issuing *hora'ah*, and did not wish to state anything from his own reasoning that he had not heard from his master. But who can confirm that if they asked him something he heard from his master, he would state it in his master's name and not anonymously? Since he was his student and 'drank of his waters,' he was not required to mention his master's name every time. If so, what proof is there that they would say [teachings] in their name?
And after a long time, the Lord enlightened my eyes and I found a conclusive proof for my words in the first chapter of *Orlah*, Mishnah 7: Rabbi Eliezer says, 'One who establishes [a plant] from the sap of *orlah* is forbidden.' Rabbi Yehoshua said to him, 'I heard explicitly,' etc. And see Rav Shoshanim Le-Dokh there, who explained that since Rabbi Eliezer did not say anything that he did not hear from his master, he did not differentiate between leaves and roots previously, for he did not hear from his master anything other than the general *din* and not the particular. And regarding this, Rabbi Yehoshua told him that he too was a student of Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai, and he had already heard the matter explicitly from his master. See there and understand. From this we learn two things, exactly as we said: We learn that the intention of 'he did not say anything that he did not hear from his master' means that he did not issue any *din* derived by *hekesh* through his own reasoning, such as this differentiation between leaves and roots, which are not fruit—a simple *din* learned from several aspects of the *din* of *orlah*, and specifically from Mishnah 9 there. And consider it well, for he was among those who feared *hora'ah*. And we learn that something he heard from his master he would say in his master's name, and he would not mention his master's name [every time, only when quoting explicitly]. Indeed, this *din* was stated in the Mishnah in the name of Rabbi Eliezer. All this is exactly as we said. My heart rejoices in the Lord.
And furthermore, even if one who refutes rejects everything he can, in the end, he will not be able to deny that these are matters entirely unconnected to what preceded them. And this is the breaking of the thread of grace that is drawn through the words of the Gaon, may his memory be a blessing. For initially he says: 'that the early Sages were not in need, etc., until the Holy Temple was destroyed,' which became nothing, except by an interruption of a new novelty: that students who did not know as their predecessors would say teachings in the name of their masters, with a proof from Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrkanos. What has the sabbatical year to do with Mount Sinai? What concern is stating a *Halakhah* in the name of his master to the matter we are discussing? And particularly for what comes after this at the conclusion of the matters, 'And therefore Rabbi was needed' or 'And that Rabbi was needed.' What connection and similarity is there with the preceding discussion that they would state a *Halakhah* in the name of their masters? By my life, I do not know what to say about the author of this version, other than that it is not enough that this Rabbi resembles those who do not learn traditions, but furthermore... and my silence is better for me. And who will not see the truth of these my words regarding this entire version, for it has entirely grown thistles and wickedness, to an astonishing degree. What is the meaning of these words, 'And that Rabbi was needed for what we have explained,' which are neither a conclusion to the preceding words, since it does not end with the phrase 'And therefore was needed,' nor a beginning of a new subject, for it says nothing after it, as indeed the subject ends there. And it is known that the phrase 'And that Rabbi was needed' is the beginning of a discourse and the presentation of a new or old reason, and we do not see such a sign here. And furthermore, what is 'for what we have explained'? And why did he not state it explicitly, in the manner of the Gaon, may his memory be a blessing, throughout the entire letter, to repeatedly clarify the subtle matter thoroughly? But the truth is, to himself, that he wished to omit 'for compilations and for clarifications,' etc., written in *Yochasin*, and he found no suitable words to put in their place, and he became afraid lest he be caught in my net. Thereupon he completed the matter by writing 'for what we have explained,' meaning that which I arranged above, which is: 'compilations of matters taught orally.' And here, the matter had already come for him to revert and change it, and therefore he replaced it with 'for what we have explained.' And this is besides the 'thistles' of 'this' in place of 'rested,' and 'and those who are in them' in place of 'two generations.' And this last error is a corruption that cannot be corrected, and I do not know, after such disgusting errors, if it is appropriate to rely on this version, even in terms of a mere appearance or scent of truth.
