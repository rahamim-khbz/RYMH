הצורך בחיבור המשנה ויתרונותיו — The Necessity and Advantages of Compiling the Mishnah
שערי תשובה סי' קפ"ז

צילום כתב היד / Scan

נוסח עברי

לן מדתנן מעשה ובדק ן' זכאי וכו' וכו' והתניא מעשה ובדק ן' זכאי (יוחסין ן' עזאי טעות מדפוס) ואסקינן תלמיד היושב לפני רבו הוה וכו', דאלמא (ח"מ ויוחסין ד"ק, אבל ד"א בעלמא ט"ס) מן ההוא זמן דהלל ושמאי, דתנו רבנן מעשה ובדק ן' זכאי בעוקצי תאנים תנייה רבי הכי במתניתין ולא שנייה" (ח"מ ויוחסין ולא תנייה ט"ס) ר"ל דכיון דרבי אחרון הוה היה ראוי להזכירו בשם רבן, ולא בשם תלמיד, אלא שלא רצה לשנות באיזה דברים הכרחיים מפני כבוד המדומה, וכמה נאה גם שמועה זאת!
"ועוד מדתנן וכו' מאברין תנן או מעברין דאלמא (ח"מ ויוחסין ד"ק, אבל ר"א ט"ס) לרבי גופיה דתרציה למתני' מספקא ליה וכו' (ז) שמעינן מינה דבלשון זה אמורה קמיה דרבי וכו', ואף מסכייתא נמי הוו מפסקן (ח"מ וכ"י שבוויין, אבל יוחסין ב' דפוסים מספקא ט"ס) מקמי דרבי מדקאמר וכו' פתח' תני לה בעוקצין דלית ליה וכו', ואיכא דוכתא דאוסיף בהו ר' פירושי וכו' לבר מן מילי דאתנינן ביומיה וביומא דבתריה, מדקאמרין זו משנה ראשונה, משנה אחרונה אמרו וכו' וכן (יוחסין. וח"מ חסרונות ושיבושים) מסכת עדויות, דאתני ביומא דאתוקם ראב"ע, דאמרינן אף עדויות נשנית בו ביום ושנינו (יוחסין, וח"מ שיבושים דבר פלא) כל בו ביום" וכו'. ועוד הפעם כיון שיצא מעט מהכוונה חזר לקשר הדברים, ואמר: "אבל מסכייתא אחרנייתא אע"ג דטעמהון הוו תנו להו כד חזי, וסתם מתני' ודאי (ח"מ) ר' מאיר, ולא (יוחסין, וח"מ טעות מלא אמרן מלבו, אלא ההיא אורחא דגמריה דר' מאיר הוה גמיר". כאן הגיע רבינו הגאון להתיר לשואל כל הג' סעיפים שבספק הא' לפרש לו האי דאמרינן סתם משנה ר"מ היא כי לא מלבו אמרן כי אם מפני שהיה תלמידו של ר"ע שהיה גם הוא לב רחב, והאריך הגאון לברר מעלת ר"ע ור"מ מן התלמוד, וכן יבאר לו שמה שאמרו ג"כ סתם תוספתא רבי נחמיה (יוחסין. וח"מ חנינה טעות ניכר) וסתם ספרא ר"י, וסתם ספרי ר"ש, וכולהו אליבא דר' עקיבא, הוא על דרך האמור בסתם משנה ר"מ שהכל הוא תורתן של ראשונים, ובדרך שאחז ר"מ תלמידו של ר"ע כן נמי בכל הברייתות והתוספתות וכו' הכל הוא תורתן של ראשונים, אלא שאותם החכמים תלמידי ר"ע, לקטום וחברום בדרך שהיה שונה להם ר' עקיבא רבם ולזה כתב: "והני ברייתא ותוספתא וספרא וספרי אילין ברייתא כולהו הוו תנו להו רבנן קמאי ואתו ר"י ור"ן ור"ש (יוחסין וח"מ חסרונות ושיבושים) ולקטינון כל חד מנייהו דיליה תוספתא ר"ן, ספרא ר"י, ספרי ר"ש, ומתני' ר"מ (יוחסין. וח"מ חסר) וכולהו אליבא דר"ע, כי כולם תלמידי ר"ע הם, אבל ברייתא אחרנייתא, לא איכפת לן

English Translation

We learn from that which is taught in a Baraita: "It once happened that Ben Zakkai examined, and so forth." And it was taught in a Baraita: "It once happened that Ben Zakkai examined," and we concluded that he was a student sitting before his teacher, and so forth. This implies that from that time of Hillel and Shammai, for the Rabbis taught: "It once happened that Ben Zakkai examined fig-stems." Rabbi taught this thus in our Mishnah, and did not alter it. That is to say, since Rabbi was a later authority, it would have been proper to mention him by the title "Rabban" and not by the title "student." However, he did not wish to alter certain essential matters out of what he perceived as respect. And how beautiful is this tradition as well!
Furthermore, we learn from that which is taught, and so forth: "Do we teach *me'aberin* (intercalate) or *ma'abrin* (make pregnant)?" This implies that even Rabbi himself, who arranged the Mishnah, was in doubt about it, and so forth. From this, we infer that it was recited before Rabbi in this very language, and so forth. And even tractates were interrupted before Rabbi, as it is stated, and so forth: "He opened these for her regarding *Uktzin* which he did not possess, and so forth." And there are places where Rabbi added interpretations, and so forth, apart from matters which were taught in his day and the day after, as they say: "This is the first Mishnah, the latter Mishnah they said, and so forth." And similarly, Tractate Eduyot, which was taught on the day that the teachings of Rabbi Akiva were established, for we say: "Even Eduyot was taught on that very day," and we have learned: "All on that very day," and so forth.
Once again, having slightly diverged from the main intent, Rabbi returned to connect the matters, and stated: "But other tractates, even though their reasoning was taught to them appropriately, the anonymous Mishnah is certainly by Rabbi Meir. And he did not state them from his own heart, but rather that was the manner of Rabbi Meir's learning which he had mastered." Here, our Gaon proceeded to permit the questioner regarding all three sections of the first doubt, to explain to him that which we say, "An anonymous Mishnah is by Rabbi Meir," is not because he stated it from his own heart, but because he was a disciple of Rabbi Akiva, who also possessed a broad understanding. And the Gaon elaborated to clarify the eminence of Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Meir from the Talmud. And similarly, he will explain to him that what they also said, "An anonymous Tosefta is by Rabbi Nehemiah," and "An anonymous Sifra is by Rabbi Yehudah," and "An anonymous Sifrei is by Rabbi Shimon," and all of them are according to the opinion of Rabbi Akiva, is in the manner stated concerning the anonymous Mishnah by Rabbi Meir, that all of it is the teaching of the early Sages. And in the manner that Rabbi Meir, a disciple of Rabbi Akiva, adopted, so too in all the Baraitot and Toseftot, and so forth, all of it is the teaching of the early Sages. However, those Sages, disciples of Rabbi Akiva, gathered and compiled them in the way that Rabbi Akiva, their teacher, taught them. And to this he wrote: "And these Baraitot, Toseftot, Sifra, and Sifrei—these are attributed to Rabbi Yehudah, Rabbi Nehemiah, and Rabbi Shimon. All these Baraitot were taught to them by the early Rabbis, and they came and gathered all of it. And the Mishnah, and all of these, are attributed to Rabbi Meir, Rabbi Yehudah (for Sifra), and Rabbi Shimon (for Sifrei), for all of them are disciples of Rabbi Akiva." But other Baraitot — what did he care about them with regard to a statute? And what did he see, and upon what did he rely? Was it to say because of "a time to act for the Lord," or for whatever other reason? And since our Gaon is silent regarding informing us of such a great matter, but rather proceeds to relate the issue of writing as a simple fact, and neither fears nor feels the need to enlighten us as to the reason, from this there is conclusive proof that from time immemorial, secret writing was not forbidden, but rather the principal prohibition was learning from written text. And this already remained until the time of the Geonim.
And as the Gaon concludes his epistle on this detail, stating: "For the Rabbis were careful to study them orally. And concerning its place, we shall further elaborate. Do not infer from this that, if so, there was no writing in the world. For if not, let us bring a Sefer Torah and examine it! (For it may be that it was oral and was not found in writing.) For we do not insist that everything was written, but rather we insist that secret writing was not forbidden, or as you might say, the prohibition was only against learning from written text. And if so, say this, or say that it was written and was lost, and remained entirely oral. Or say that there were two written versions: one from those who read 'ayin' as 'aleph,' like the Ashkenazim, and one from those who differentiate between 'ayin' and 'aleph,' like the Sephardim who went into exile among the descendants of Shem, and not among the descendants of Yefet, the Ashkenazim and French." And we have already demonstrated below that even today, when everything is written in a book, we do not use the term "writing" concerning the Oral Torah, but rather terms like "repetition" (*shinnun*) and "we learned" (*tenan*), and the like. And indeed, the term "shinnun" has become the standard idiom for the Oral Torah, and no proof may be brought from it. And if so, say that there were two written versions before Rabbi, and both are properly interpreted. And concerning this, there was a doubt as to which was the primary and foundational one. And all this can be explained smoothly without difficulty, for our Gaon's entire intention in citing this Mishnah is solely to clarify that Rabbi not only did not invent any law from his own heart, but rather he was meticulous in teaching his Mishnah (in matters where he saw the necessity not to alter it) precisely as the early Sages had taught. And these matters are clear as a garment. And further below, in the fourth section, we shall further discuss this detail.
