נוסח עברי

המורם מכל האמור כי להיות איסור לימוד דברים שבע"פ מתוך הכתב רבוע על התנאים ועל האמוראים, ע"ז איך אפשר שיאמרו בפומבי ניתי ס"ת ונחזי אם הענין להם סוד גדול יותר מסוד העיבור ולא היו החמדות גנוזות כ"א ביד גדולי הדור או תלמידיהם הנאמנים אשר יכירו בהם סימנים ראויים לכך. וכבר הבאתי לעיל כי זהו שאנו מוצאים בריו"ח נפק תניה לתורת כהנים בתלתא יומי וסברא בתלתא ירחי, היכן נפק, וממי תנייה? אם לא היתה בכתב, והל"ל, "תנייה מר' פלוני" כדאשכחן בכתובות ד"ן ע"א תנא מיניה דר"י בר חנינא שר"ל שלמד ממנו בע"פ, אם לא שמוכרח דנפק למקום המשומר ששם גנוז לו הת"כ ותנייה בתורה שבע"פ בג' ימים כק"ש, ואח"כ היה מסביר אותה במשך ג' ירחים והוא פשיטות הענין, וכן בגיטין דמ"ד ע"א על בעיא דעבד שהפיל עצמו לגייסות, אמר ליה ר' ירמיה לר' זריקא פוק עיין במכלתיך, נפק דק ואשכח דתניא וכו', וכאן רש"י לפי דרכו כבר נמצא במצור ובמצוק, ופי' עיין במכלתיך במשנה הסדורה לך לידע מה נשיב וכמעט הוציא הדברים מידי פשיטותן, למה אמר פוק? ולהיכן יצא? וכן ר' זריקא למה נפק? ולאיזה מקום? ולמה יאמר עיין במכלתיך? ולא עיין והשב תשו' ופשוט בעיא זו? אם לא שתאמר דמנהגם כמנהגינו היום שכשנפל לנו ספק בדין אחד אנו מעיינים בדינים הדומים להם ומדמים מילתא למלתא, או בהצד השוה או בק"ו, כן היו חכמי המשנה והתלמוד בהנהגת תלמודם אלא דאנחנו היום מותרים בפומבי להיות לנו ספרים וללמוד מתוכם, והם היו אסורים להיות להם ספרים פומביים של תושע"פ, ואפילו בחשאי היו אסורים ללמוד מתוכם כי אם ראיה בעלמא, וזהירין רבנן למגרסינהו על פה, וכן ההיא דא"ל רב חסדא לרבה בב"מ דח"י ע"ב על ספק דב' יוסף ן' שמעון פוק עיין בה, דלאורתא בעי מינך רב הונא, נפק דק ואשכח, דתנן כל מעשה ב"ד וכו', ודוק קורא חביב! דכל מקום שנמצא לשון "פוק" או "נפק דק ואשכח" מביאים בידם משנה או ברייתא, או לקיחת דברים בע"פ, וזה אות ומופת דלא עאלו ונפקו כדי להתבודד ולמצוא סברא, כ"א לחפש הסתרים ולחזור על משנתם אולי יוכלו למצוא דמיון להתרת ספקם, וכן ההיא דיבמות דס"א ע"ב נפק ומצא הברייתא במחלוקת ר"מ ור"י, ובזה פי' סברת ר' אליעזר עי"ש כי לקצר אני צריך, ואם שלדעת רש"י וסיעת מרחמוהי יתפרש הכל על העיון וההתבודדות, אין מי שיוכל להכחיש כי יותר מרווח פירוש הגאונים כפשטן של דברים מפי' ז"ל, ולו יהי כי שקולים הם ויבואו שניהם, חזרנו לדמעיקרא גברא אגברא קא רמית? ואחר שכ"ע מודים, ר"ל, כל ב' הכתות, מעידין בגדלן דלכל אפיא היו אסורים הלמוד מתוך הכתב, א"כ בהכרח נפלו טעיות אגב שיטפא דגירסא, כאשר כתב רש"י בחולין דע"ד ע"ב ד"ה חלבו דמאי, ועיין תוס' שם בריש העמוד. ובעין לזה כתב התיו"ט בפ"ב דמס' מכות על ריש משנה ח' כיוצא בו וע"ז התנא מסרך סריך בלישניה ממשנה ח' דפרק בתרא דשביעית ששם שייך נוסח זה דהתם קאי אדלעיל מיניה ולא הכא עי"ש, וכל זה גרמא השינון בע"פ, ובהכרח אמר ר' חייא "מתנינא" ולא "וכתיבנא" ובהכרח היו צריכין לסימנים המפוזרים היום בתלמודנו, ובהכרח ג"כ נמצא בו קיבוץ כמה ענינים שונים זה מזה יחד, אם בעבור בעל המימרא, אם בעבור ההתדמות בלשון ובסגנון, והכל יסוב באמת כדי להקל על הזכרון ולסייעו סיוע שיש בו ממש כיון דסוף סוף היו אסורים ללמוד מתוך הכתב, והכתב לא היה נמצא להם בגלוי בבית המדרש, ואם ישכח הרב המלמד לתלמידיו בו בפרק אין תרופה למכתו, ובהכרח צריך לקום ולסגור בית המדרש לפי שעה, 'ואם יחזור ויבא תכף לבית המדרש תגלה ותראה לעין כל, כי נפק דק בספרים הגנוזים ואשכח, ולפי ערך האיסור היה הרב בו בפרק כהולך רכיל מגלה סוד, ע"כ אלין דהוו

English Translation

From all that has been stated, given that the prohibition against studying matters of the Oral Law from written texts was firmly established for the Tannaitic Sages and the elevated Amoraic Sages, how could they have publicly declared, "Let us bring a Sefer Torah and examine," if the matter was a greater secret to them than the secret of the lunar calendar intercalation? Furthermore, these precious matters were not concealed except in the hands of the leading Sages of the generation or their trustworthy disciples, in whom appropriate signs for such responsibility could be discerned.
I have already demonstrated above that this is what we find concerning Rabbi Yochanan, who went forth and studied Torat Kohanim in three days, and its underlying reasoning in three months. Where did he go forth, and from whom did he learn it? If it was not in written form, the text should have stated, "He learned it from so-and-so," as we find in Tractate Ketubot 54a, "He learned from Rabbi Yannai bar Chanina," meaning he learned from him orally. This necessitates that he went out to a guarded location where Torat Kohanim was concealed for his use, and he learned it as part of the Oral Law in three days, similar to the recitation of the Keriat Shema, and thereafter he would expound upon it over a period of three months. This, indeed, is the straightforward understanding of the matter.
And similarly, in Tractate Gittin 44a, regarding the question of one who submitted himself to foreign legions, Rabbi Yirmiyah said to Rabbi Zera, "Go forth and examine your written compendia." He went forth, investigated, and discovered that it had been taught, and so forth. Here, Rashi, following his particular method, finds himself in a predicament, explaining "examine your written compendia" as referring to the Mishnah arranged for you, in order to discern the proper response. This interpretation almost entirely removes the words from their straightforward meaning. Why did he say, "Go forth"? And to where did he go? Similarly, why did Rabbi Zera go forth? And to what destination? And why would he say, "Examine your written compendia," rather than simply, "Examine and give a Teshuvah and resolve this question"? Unless one posits that their custom was like our custom today: when a doubt arises concerning a particular Halakhah, we examine analogous Halakhot and compare one matter to another, either through a common attribute or by an *a fortiori* argument. So, too, did the Sages of the Mishnah and the Talmud conduct their studies. The distinction is that we today are publicly permitted to possess books and study from them, whereas they were forbidden to possess public written texts of the Oral Torah; and even in private, they were forbidden to study from them, except for a mere visual reference, as the Rabbis were scrupulous in memorizing them orally.
And similarly, the incident in Tractate Bava Metzia 18b, where Rav Chisda said to Rabbah concerning the doubt regarding two individuals named Yosef ben Shimon, "Go forth and examine it, for this evening Rav Huna will inquire of you." He went forth, investigated, and found that it is taught, "All actions of a Beit Din," and so forth. Observe, dear reader, that wherever the expression "go forth" or "went forth, investigated, and found" appears, it signifies the consultation of a Mishnah or a Baraita in their possession, or the reception of oral traditions. This serves as a clear indication and proof that they did not merely go to and fro to engage in idle speculation and devise a novel reasoning, but rather to seek out recondite matters and to review their oral teachings, hoping to find an analogy for the resolution of their doubt.
And similarly, the incident in Tractate Yevamot 61b, where he went forth and found the Baraita concerning the dispute between Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel. With this, the reasoning of Rabbi Eliezer is clarified; consult the text there, for brevity compels me to move on. Even if, according to Rashi and his sympathetic adherents, all this is to be interpreted as referring to intense study and solitary contemplation, no one can deny that the interpretation of the Geonim, in its plain sense, is more comprehensive than that of Rashi. And even if they were deemed equivalent and both interpretations were to be accepted, we would merely revert to the original difficulty: "Are you pitting one man against another?"
And since all agree—that is to say, both schools of thought, in their esteemed wisdom, attest that in all aspects, study from written texts was forbidden—it follows inevitably that errors occurred due to the extensive oral recitation. As Rashi wrote in Tractate Chullin 74b, under the lemma "Its fat is what...," and see Tosefot there at the beginning of the page. To this effect, the Tosefot Yom Tov wrote in the second chapter of Tractate Makkot on the beginning of Mishnah 8, similarly, that because of this, the Tanna entangles his language from Mishnah 8 of the last chapter of Tractate Shevi'it, where this particular wording belongs, as it refers to the preceding material and not to the current context; consult that passage. All of this resulted from oral memorization.
And, necessarily, Rabbi Chiya declared, "We teach," and not "We write." Necessarily, too, they required the mnemonic devices that are today dispersed throughout our Talmud. And, inevitably, we also find therein the compilation of several disparate matters together, whether on account of the author of a particular saying, or due to a similarity in language and style. All of this truly served to facilitate memory and provide substantial assistance, given that they were ultimately forbidden to study from written texts, and such texts were not openly available to them in the study hall. Should the Rabbi teaching his disciples forget a point in that chapter, there would be no remedy for his predicament. Necessarily, he would then have to rise and temporarily close the study hall. Should he immediately return to the study hall, it would become manifest and apparent to all that he had gone forth, investigated in the concealed books, and found what he sought. In proportion to the gravity of the prohibition, the Rabbi in that circumstance would be akin to a talebearer revealing a secret. Therefore, these were those who strove to uphold their conviction, and for this reason, their witnesses were called by a certain Tanna to testify concerning their misguided belief; observe this carefully.
And by the way, I shall state the obligation incumbent upon every seeker of wisdom, in whatever branch of wisdom it may be. It is this: It is not propitious to continuously employ the instruments entrusted to us for the purpose of managing the wisdom we possess, for through such constant usage, it will gradually be inverted, akin to an altered seal, and soon we shall perceive it no more, and new visages will have appeared, while the former ones will be consigned to oblivion. So, too, I declare: although the authority has been granted to us, by those who served the Geonim, such as Rabbeinu Chananel, to interpret certain matters as we wish—for they bear no relation to the established Halakhah—nevertheless, it is not advisable to avail oneself of this authority overly much. For indeed, its potential detriment for those who turn to it far outweighs its benefit, and one should not employ it even once in a Jubilee year concerning a matter for which there are a thousand rational proofs and established principles of wisdom. This is especially true when the ancient interpretation is as unyielding as iron, and all the legions of Israel have grappled with its difficulty, yet a solitary alternative presents itself, sweet as a honeycomb. This much suffices for those who cherish wisdom for its own sake.
