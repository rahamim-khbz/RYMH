היתר כתיבת תושבע"פ ולימודה: 'לא אכתיבו אלא תרוצי הוו מתרצי' — Permissibility of Writing and Studying Oral Law: 'Not Written, but Arranged'
שערי תשובה סי' קפ"ז

צילום כתב היד / Scan

נוסח עברי

אופן, עכ"פ מצא גאוננו עצמו מחוייב לברר וללבן לפני השואלים באר היטב שידעו נאמנה החיוב הגדול שהיה מוטל על ישראל ועל רבנן לגרוס הדברים בע"פ שלא מן הכתב, ומתוך דברי השואלים מוכח להדיא דנדמה להם שחיבור המשנה והתלמוד היו ככל חיבורי זמננו שנכתבים ונחתמים לאלתר אדעתא ללמוד מתוכם, וע"ז היה החיוב מוטל על הגאון ז"ל להוציאם מטעותם הלזה, ולברר להם ולומר: "ודכתבתון כיצד נכתבה המשנה ונכתב התלמוד, תלמוד ומשנה לא אכתיבו אלא תרוצי הוו מתרצי, וזהירין רבנן למגרסינהו ע"פ אבל מנסחי דכתיבי" (כד) דאמרינן בתמורה דברים שבע"פ א"א רשאי לאומרם בכתב, שנא' כי על פי הדברים האלה כרתי ברית, אלה אתה כותב, וא אתה כותב הלכות" כונתו מבוארת להשיב להם ולומר: זה ששמעתם שהיה כתיבה, כי לא אפשר בלאו הכי היינו דוקא לתרץ הדברים ולסדרם בלשון ופה אחד, ולאחר את המאוחר" ולהקדים את המוקדם, וכדומה ממלאכת הסידור והחיבור והדומה להם, אבל תדעו נאמנה שמעולם לא אכתיבו ר"ל לא ניתנו ליכתב כדי שילמדו מתוכם כי זה באיסורא קאי עד תחילת זמן הגאונים ואפי' שהיה הכל כתוב ומסודר ומחובר הוו זהירין למגרסינהו על פה אבל מנסחי דכתיבי ר"ל דמעולם לא היו לומדים מתוך הכתב, ואפי' בדרך עיון בעלמא אלא היו רואים מתוך הכתב, ולוקחים הדברים על פה כדי שאחר שהיה שנון בע"פ חומריות הדברים בלי הבנה כלל, אבל שנונים היטב בע"פ כק"ש, אחר כן היו מעיינים בהם ומסברים אותם ומפלפלים בהם, כמו שעשה ר"יוח דתני תו"כ בתלתא יומי, וסברא בתלתא ירחי, וזהו ממש אומרו: "למגרסינהו ע"פ, אבל מנוסחי דכתיבי" וע"ז שייך שפיר אומרו "וזהירין הוו רבנן" דבאמת לשון זהירות אינו שייך אלא כשהמכשול לפניו, וזה המכשול לא יתכן אם לא היתה הכתיבה לפניהם, שהוא המכשול האמתי שמא עי"ז יבא ללמוד מתוך הכתב, אבל אם אין כתיבה בעולם אין מכשול ומאי "זהירין" דקאמר? ואלו לא היו זהירין מאי הוי? הרי אין כתיבה לפניהם! ובעל כרחם למגרסינהו על פה, וע"ז שפיר הביא ראיה מרן הגאון ז"ל מהתלמוד שעיקר האיסור הוא: "דברים שבע"פ א"א רשאי לאומרם בכתב" שר"ל עיקר האיסור הוא האמירה מתוך הכתב, ולא הכתיבה בלי לימוד מתוכה, וע"ז סיים להביא מאי דתנא דבי ר"יש "אלה אתה כותב ואי אתה כותב הלכות" שכבר הארכנו לעיל בעיקר ביאור הסוגיאות הללו דהך דריב"ן, והך דתנא דבי רי"ש כחדא נפקין וכחדא שריין, ולא מתפרשין לעלמין דכמו שדרשת ריב"ן לא איירי כי אם באמירת דברים שבע"פ מתוך הכתב, ולא על איסור כתיבה, כך תנא דבי ר"יש לא איירי כ"א על איסור אמירת דברים מתוך הכתב, ולא על איסור הכתיבה כלל,

English Translation

Be that as it may, our esteemed Gaon found it incumbent upon himself to clarify and elucidate thoroughly for the questioners the profound obligation resting upon the people of Israel and upon the Rabbis to recite the traditions orally, rather than from written texts. It is explicitly evident from the questioners' remarks that they erroneously assumed the compilation of the Mishnah and the Talmud to have been akin to contemporary literary works, which are immediately committed to writing and sealed with the express purpose of subsequent study from their pages. Hence, the revered Gaon, of blessed memory, was compelled to disabuse them of this misconception and to clarify, stating: "As for your query concerning the manner in which the Mishnah and the Talmud were committed to writing, know that neither the Talmud nor the Mishnah was actually written down for study; rather, they were orally organized and arranged. The Rabbis exercised caution to recite them orally, though they did consult written drafts." This aligns with the dictum found in Tractate Temurah: "Matters transmitted orally may not be uttered from a written text, for it is stated: 'For according to the tenor of these words I have made a covenant with thee.' It further says: 'These words shalt thou write,' implying that 'these thou mayest write, but thou mayest not write Halakhot.'"
His intent is clearly to respond to them as follows: What you have heard regarding the existence of a written form – for such a measure was indispensable – pertained exclusively to the task of arranging the various traditions and ordering them into a uniform expression, to postpone the later and to precede the earlier elements, and to similar aspects of editorial and compositional work. Yet, be assured that they were never committed to writing – that is to say, they were not permitted to be written down for the purpose of study therefrom, as this was prohibited until the inception of the Geonic era. And even when everything had been written down, arranged, and redacted, the Rabbis were scrupulous in reciting these traditions orally. This implies that they never engaged in study from the written text, not even for a cursory review. Instead, they would consult written drafts merely to perceive the content, then commit the material to memory orally. Thus, once the bare facts of the matter were thoroughly impressed upon their oral memory – without initial comprehension, but precisely as one memorizes the Shema prayer – they would subsequently engage in contemplation, explication, and dialectical discussion of these subjects. This practice is exemplified by Rabbi Yochanan, who reportedly mastered the Torat Kohanim text in three days, yet devoted three months to comprehend its meaning. This, then, is precisely the import of his statement: "to recite them orally, but from written drafts."
In this context, his declaration, "and the Rabbis exercised caution," is entirely apposite. Indeed, the term "caution" is meaningfully employed only when a potential pitfall lies before one. This particular pitfall—that one might come to study from a written text—could not exist unless written material was available to them, which is the true stumbling block. But if no written texts existed in the world, there would be no occasion for caution. What, then, would be the significance of "exercising caution" if there were no written texts before them? They would be compelled to recite orally in any event. Appropriately, our esteemed Master the Gaon, of blessed memory, adduced proof from the Talmud that the fundamental prohibition lies in the dictum: "Matters transmitted orally may not be uttered from a written text." This signifies that the primary prohibition concerns the recitation from a written source, rather than the mere act of writing itself without subsequent study from that text. To this point, he concluded by citing the teaching from the School of Rabbi Ishmael: "These thou mayest write, but thou mayest not write Halakhot." As we have previously elaborated at length regarding the fundamental interpretation of these passages – that of Rabbi Yochanan and that of the School of Rabbi Ishmael – they are to be understood as emerging from and resolved by a single principle, never to be separated. For just as Rabbi Yochanan’s exposition refers exclusively to the oral recitation of traditions from written text, rather than to a prohibition against the act of writing itself, so too, the teaching of the School of Rabbi Ishmael concerns solely the prohibition against reciting traditions from written text, and not at all the prohibition against the mere act of writing.
Moreover, in a variant reading from the manuscript of Chamesh Machot, the author here altered the text by adding and omitting elements. The text reads: "As for your query concerning the manner in which the Mishnah and the Talmud were committed to writing, know that neither the Talmud nor the Mishnah was actually written down; rather, they were organized and arranged. The Rabbis exercised caution to recite them orally, but from drafts, *they did not*," where the word "d'khetivi" (that were written) was omitted and "lo" (not) was inserted. Through this emendation, the copyist has brought the entire responsa into alignment with the view of our French Rabbinic authorities, who held that no trace of written transmission existed until the dawn of the Geonic period. It is also intended to demonstrate how, even in this instance, the "scribal hand" is clearly discernible to all, requiring no further elaboration. For, apart from our previous arguments, specifically, "What is the meaning of 'exercising caution'?"—a term inapplicable if no written texts existed—this observation alone suffices to bolster our authentic interpretation: that written texts did exist. Consequently, this variant reading is unequivocally deemed a scribal error, beyond any shadow of a doubt.
A further difficulty arises: If the Gaon had already stated that the traditions were not committed to writing at all—and, according to the proponent of this variant reading, this implies a complete absence of any written material, rather than merely a prohibition against studying from it—then what is the necessity of his subsequent remark, "but from drafts, *not*"? If nothing at all was written down, from whence would any drafts be found for the Gaon to elaborate so extensively, instructing: "to recite orally," but "from drafts, *not*"? Clearly, this cannot be the case, given the premise that they were not written at all. Even an ordinary meticulous speaker would not utter such superfluous remarks, much less our esteemed Gaon, whose every utterance is deemed invaluable.
However, according to our aforementioned interpretation, even this variant reading, despite its omission of the word "d'khetivi" (that were written), retains a foundational truth. For the initial clarification, "they were not written," should be understood as "they were not permitted to be written for study"—implying that while written versions of the Talmuds existed, they were not intended for direct learning. The Gaon concludes by stating that despite the existence of written texts, the Rabbis were nevertheless meticulous in reciting orally, "but from drafts, *not*," signifying their caution against studying from the written text itself. This, then, clarifies his initial statement, "they were not written." Ponder this carefully.
In my estimation, the authentic reading is likely: "but from written drafts, *not*," meaning that they were to recite orally and not from a book. It is possible that in the version found in the Sefer Yuchasin, the word "not" was inadvertently omitted; who can truly know? Ultimately, this distinction is not critical, as both interpretations converge on a single understanding. If we adopt the reading "but from written drafts," as it appears in the Sefer Yuchasin before us, its intent is clearly, as stated above, that they would consult the written text merely to perceive the content and then recite it orally. If, however, the reading is "but from written drafts, *not*," it is to be interpreted as previously explained: that they were cautious not to study from the written text, not even a single letter, but rather only to glance at it for the purpose of committing the material to oral memory.
