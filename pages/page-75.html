The following lines illuminate how our Sages, of blessed memory, are accustomed to use the expression "not given to be written" concerning something where our primary regard is not for the essence of the writing itself, whether it was written or not written, but rather for its purpose, or for an external cause resulting from that action. For if, through the material act of writing, some inner or outer benefit accrues to us, then certainly it will be said "it is given to be written," and it is fitting that it has come into existence. And conversely, if the material act has no intended purpose, then certainly it will be said, "not given to be written," and it is as if it never came into existence.
Similarly, regarding the Oral Torah, even though it is written and hidden away, it is called "not given to be written," for without this, one cannot learn from the writing. And this is what you will find in the Me'iri's introduction to his work *Beit ha-Bechirah*, where he wrote as follows: "It is stated explicitly in the fifth chapter of Gittin that some Sages wished to prohibit the reading of Haggadah books on Shabbat, and also their handling, since they were not given to be written, for a person is not permitted to put oral matters into writing. But their great Sages agreed to permit it, and they brought the reason for this permission from the verse, 'It is time to act for the Lord' (Psalms 119:126), meaning, since learning was not successful by other means, it is given to be written. And this is what our Sages, of blessed memory, said: 'Since it is impossible, "it is time to act for the Lord, they have violated Your Torah."'"
And in a similar vein, he wrote in his novellae to Tractate Shabbat, up to folio 115, as follows: "And so too the Ge'onim wrote that in our time we may rescue Haggadah books and Talmudic books, for all of them are now given to be written, as they stated in the fifth chapter of Gittin, on account of 'It is time to act for the Lord.' And in Tractate Temurah, Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Laqish were studying a book of Aggadah. They said, 'It is better that the Torah be uprooted than that the Torah be forgotten.'"
We learn from the words of the Me'iri, of blessed memory, that even though he, of blessed memory, in his introduction to Avot, proclaims forcefully several times that the writing of the Oral Torah was never forbidden and was customary from the days of the Men of the Great Assembly, nevertheless he states that from the time of the Ge'onim onward it was 'given to be written' and we may rescue it. And he is supported by the two *sugyot* from Temurah and Gittin, and they were not hidden from his holy eyes, just as they were not hidden from Rabbi Shmuel ha-Nagid and the Rambam. And how can his words be sustained unless the intent of "given to be written" or "not given to be written" does not refer to the act of writing itself, whether it is permitted or forbidden, but rather to the prohibition of learning from it and its permission? For at the time when learning from writing was prohibited, from the days of the Men of the Great Assembly until the Rabbanan Savora'ei, it was called "not given to be written," meaning that the writing was a futile act, inasmuch as we could not learn from it, and therefore it was forbidden to handle it on Shabbat. And from the time of the Rabbanan Savora'ei, when learning from it was permitted due to necessity, because learning was not successful by other means, the act of writing thus became an essential act, and we apply to it "given to be written," and it is permitted to handle them on Shabbat. And this necessity began in the time of Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Laqish for books of Aggadah, because there was not enough time for them to be acquired orally, and hidden away without oral transmission, and without learning from them, they would be as if forgotten with no escape. For this, the Perushim permitted the matter, and from that day on, it would be said of books of Aggadah that they were "given to be written." And from the time of Rav Yosef, some of the Halakhot, as an unusual matter, were permitted to be learned from writing for Rav Yosef, and they were called "given to be written." And in the time of the Rabbanan Savora'ei, when the entire Oral Torah was permitted to be learned from writing, and for us to have books, it is like the Written Torah; this is "given to be written," and that is "given to be written," without any hairsbreadth of difference, since a great and holy purpose exists for the compilations of the Oral Torah today, namely, learning from them, and this is impossible without the means of writing, and this is clear.
And in Sefer ha-Pardes by Rashi, concerning the laws of Rosh Hashanah, it is written explicitly, as follows: "And the holy and watchful one, our elder, Rabbi Binyamin bar Shmuel of Constanza, said: When our Sages shortened the eighteen (blessings) to seven, there was much wisdom in Israel, and they only needed to preach, not to compose *piyyutim*. But since wisdom had already diminished and declined, they established *piyyut* in place of *midrash*, etc. Even though our Rabbis shortened the eighteen (blessings) to seven, we need not be concerned about the burden on the congregation for a mitzvah, because of 'It is time to act for the Lord,' etc. For Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Laqish were studying a book of Aggadah on Shabbat, and they said, 'But was it not given to be written?' Rather, 'It is time to act for the Lord' – to preach and teach the world, and it is impossible to explain orally, for the angel of forgetfulness is prevalent, and they preach in writing even though it was not given to be written (meaning, 'It is time to act for the Lord' permits this). And they were not concerned about extending the *piyyut* on Shabbat due to the burden on the congregation."
I have also used the words of this ancient Rabbi to demonstrate that the primary meaning of the expression "not given to be written" was coined solely to indicate the prohibition of learning and expounding from writing, and not concerning the writing itself. And so too is implied by the expression of the Ra'avyah, cited in the Aggudah in chapter "Ein Dorshin be-Arayot be-Shloshah," concerning the words "and they asserted in the days of." If these are Rashi's words, then we would know who Rashi's elder was, and it is proper for those engaged in critical textual analysis to search if the name of this Rabbi is mentioned elsewhere.
