English Translation

The sum of the matters is that the essence of the *Beraitot* are those taught in the school of Rabbi Chiya and Rabbi Oshaya, and also those which the Talmud meticulously examined and upon which it relied. And with all this lengthy discussion, a conclusive answer emerged for the *Gaon*, *zikhrono livrakhah*, to the third query in all its three sections, and he clarified everything for them. And this means that the *Beraita* was arranged in the days of Rabbi, and that it was not possible for Rabbi, according to the measure of his concise and precise work, to expand the matters further, and that the core body of the *Beraitot* are indeed the teaching of the *Rishonim*, each and every one having taught them at length and in breadth, according to his own method, power, and idiom. And we had great need of the work of Rabbi Chiya and Rabbi Oshaya. And after our *Gaon* clarified all this, shining like the noonday sun, and all the complexities of the third query were smoothed out, this very matter will lead the *Gaon* to awaken those who slumber. And he sought to arouse them regarding what appeared from their query, that they had reserved their question concerning the Talmud for last, inquiring how it was written. And we have already explained above that this query is divided into its four sections, just as the first query, "How was the Mishnah written?", is divided; where we explained that if the Mishnah is the teaching of the *Rishonim* from the Men of the Great Assembly, why are the opinions of the *Acharonim* mentioned therein, and similarly regarding the other sections. So too is the query concerning the Talmud: if it is the teaching of the *Rishonim*, why is it referred to by the name of *Acharonim*? And if the Talmud is *Acharon*, then it is not *Rishon*. And that is to say, if so, all the discussions therein are late in time, and the *Rishonim* had nothing of it. Regarding this, the *Gaon*, *zikhrono livrakhah*, came forth like a hero to banish this error from the midst of the questioner, and he completed his words and wrote: "And concerning the Talmud also, about which you have inquired," that is to say, and it appears from your words that it is late in time; this is not so, for even the earliest *Rishonim* possessed a Talmud. Rather, their arrangement was not uniform, for each one taught his disciples all the matters contained in the Talmud (which is the *Gemara*) in a manner different from his fellow, precisely as was the case with the fundamental laws of the Mishnah, where each one taught in a language and order different from his fellow. So too was the matter precisely with the reasons for the Mishnah, the dialectical discussions, and the legal cases; each one had a different manner and order, but all the rivers flow to one law and one reason. And he elaborated with demonstrable proofs, saying: "And concerning the Talmud, about which you have inquired, even the earliest *Rishonim* possessed a Talmud, for we say, and so forth, and their fundamental principles were minor and major, and so forth, and so forth." And many such matters. And when the *Rishonim* would state their *Halakhah*, each one as he studied it, these matters constituted their Talmud, to reveal the reasons for the Torah, as we say in *Eruvin*, and so forth. And so too they possessed other aspects of their Talmud, to discern novel interpretations, and so forth. And from where do we know that they possessed a Talmud? For concerning Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakkai they said that he had studied up to the dialectical discussions of Abaye and Rava, and so forth. And after Rabbi established the *Halakhah* and his Mishnah, that constituted their Talmud, in which they explained the precise points of the Mishnah, of the homiletics, as our Rabbis taught, and they detailed and generalized and provided its reasons for the Mishnah, and so forth. And they said concerning it: "This matter itself is difficult," and so forth. Rabbi Yochanan said: "In the days of Rabbi, this Mishnah was taught," and so forth. He then expounded to them, and so forth, and the Talmud is.
And I wonder at one who writes matters before the Sages of Israel, who are capable of discerning whether they are sound or not, how his heart can be filled to trespass into a domain not his own and to determine matters that are not so. Primarily, who would rely upon such a poor, impoverished, and utterly erroneous version as that of this *Ba'al ha-Chofesh*? And he who erred in copying below on page 30, writing Rabbi Akiva instead of Rabbi Zeira, all the more so will he err in writing Rabbi Akiva instead of Rabbi. And there are thousands upon thousands of such errors without number. And if so, even if his version in this matter seemed plausible, it is not proper to treat it as a primary source, all the more so, and *kal va-chomer*, since it is a complete error, substituting Rabbi Akiva for Rabbi. Firstly, what was there in the days of Rabbi Akiva that this Mishnah was taught, to the extent that everyone abandoned the Mishnah and followed the *Gemara*? And were they not in those days learning only the laws of kings and their wars, until Rabbi Akiva erred and said, "This is the Messiah King"? And even though he had many thousands of disciples, nevertheless, we do not find in the Talmud that in his time there was a greater need for Talmud than for Mishnah. Not so in the time of Rabbi, when the founts of wisdom were opened and they occupied themselves with compiling the Mishnah and arranging it with wondrous wisdom, such that in concise terms it would encompass everything. And truly, from this wondrous work, they discerned the need for dialectical discussion, for logical analysis, and for understanding one matter from another, in order to come to inherit such an unconstrained heritage. For had Rabbi and his court not been sharp and astute and proficient in the methods of Talmudic discourse, and possessed of great expertise, he would not have been able to produce such a perfect and concise compilation with ease. And for this they abandoned the Mishnah and followed the *Gemara*. And for this, it is perfectly appropriate to teach that in the days of Rabbi, the Mishnah was taught, and not in the days of Rabbi Akiva, for in the days of Rabbi Akiva, no act occurred that would foster this yearning in any way. And further, from the language of the *Beraita* itself, which the *Gaon* brought forth in its form, it is clearly proven therefrom that it was in the days of Rabbi and not Rabbi Akiva, for it says: "He then expounded to them." And if Rabbi Akiva taught the first Mishnah, and Rabbi the later Mishnah, as the *Gaon* himself wrote: "Rabbi then expounded to them" – what does "then" imply?
