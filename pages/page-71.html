מגלה, כי מגלה אינה אלא דף אחד, או ספר קטן, כמו שהן חמש מגלות, וכמו שהיא מגלת תענית, וכן משמע ממה שנחלקו אם התורה חתומה נתנה או מגלה מגלה נתנה ואמנם המשנה כלה, ובפרט בימי איסי בן יהודה שהיה בימי רבינו הקדוש (ט"ל) היתה ספר גדול יותר מספר תורה, ואיך תקרא מגלה? ומלבד כל זה, הנה באלפי מקומות נזכרו בתלמוד משניות חוץ מאותן שסידר רבי, והן כל הברייתות הידועות בלשון תניא, ותנו רבנן וזולתם, ובכמה מקומות נזכרו מגלות סתרים? הלוא הם בטלים באלף או ברבוא, הלא דבר הוא האין זו ראיה ברורה, כי ההלכות הכתובות כמו זר נחשבו, ואינן אלא דבר היוצא מן הכלל דרך זרות, לא שהיו כל התנאים או רובם כותבים משניות, אלא ממש כמו שפי' רש"י

English Translation

A scroll, for a scroll is nothing but a single sheet or a small book, just as there are the Five Megillot, and just as there is Megillat Ta'anit. And likewise, it is implied from their dispute concerning whether the Torah was given as a sealed whole or given scroll by scroll. But the entire Mishnah, especially in the days of Isi ben Yehudah, who lived in the time of Rabbenu HaKadosh, was a larger book than a Sefer Torah. How then could it be called a scroll?
And besides all this, behold, in thousands of places in the Talmud, Mishnayot are mentioned other than those arranged by Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi. These are all the known Baraitot, introduced with the expressions *tanya* ("it has been taught") and *tanu Rabbanan* ("our Rabbis have taught"), and others besides. And in how many places are "secret scrolls" mentioned? Are they not annulled in a thousand or in ten thousand? Is this not something? Is this not clear proof that the written Halakhot were considered a rarity and were merely an exceptional phenomenon, an oddity? Not that all or most of the Tannaim wrote Mishnayot, but rather precisely as Rabbi Shelomo Yitzchaki explained.
The respondent stated regarding the two proofs, "I shall address them," and also regarding the third, which is the first from what Rabbi Yochanan wrote: "The Torah, a minority of which is written, and the majority of which is oral." I shall not withhold my speech, for there is no Midrash without novelty. Let us first state what we have already written sufficiently on this matter previously: that the intent of "a minority written" means a minority of the obligation to learn it from a written text, and "the majority oral" refers to the majority of the obligation to recite it orally.
And this is precisely apparent from the words of Rabbi Yochanan himself, whose words are brought in Shir HaShirim Rabbah, Chapter 4, Section 4: "For according to these words, the Written Torah and the Oral Torah, I have made a covenant with you. And if you violate it and render what is oral into writing, and what is written into oral transmission, you will not receive reward. Because thus have I given it: Torah as written, and Torah as oral." This means: to learn matters of the Written Torah orally, after you possess a Sefer Torah, as I commanded you in a specific commandment to write a Sefer Torah, based on the verse, "Now therefore write ye this song for you." Yet, despite this, you learn matters orally – this is forbidden to you. And likewise, the reverse: "what is oral as written." This means that you learn matters of the Oral Torah from a hidden text that you possess for memory's sake. Concerning this, I did not command you, nor did it occur to me to forbid its secret writing for a time of need, such as forgetfulness. Because of this, it might occur to you to lighten the burden of receiving matters orally and instead learn from a written text. Therefore, I command you today to receive these matters orally and not to learn from the hidden text which is useful only for memory.
And if you transgress and act contrary to all that has been stated and violate it, not only will you incur punishment as one who violates the word of the Lord, but also you will not receive reward, for sometimes a commandment performed through a transgression may still earn reward, as written by HaRav Kaphtan Yashir Peh Tehillah there, see what is written there. This entire statement should not be interpreted as referring to the prohibition of writing, nor to the prohibition of learning from a written text. For besides the plain meaning of the words that states, "and if you violate," etc., which is truly a matter and its opposite: Concerning matters of the Written Torah, the situation is that there is a written text, and moreover, it is a commandment to write a Sefer Torah. The essential new prohibition is to learn from it and not orally, and it does not come to address the commandment of writing a Sefer Torah.
So too, similarly, "what is oral as written" means that there is a hidden text, and the prohibition is not to learn from it except orally. And just as it does not speak of Written Torah matters to obligate their writing or to introduce a new commandment for writing sacred texts, so too concerning Oral Torah matters, it does not speak of prohibiting their writing or introducing a new negative commandment merely because they are written in a book. And besides all this, from the words of Rabbi Yehudah bar Il'ai, who adds to Rabbi Yochanan's prohibition of violating the covenant, it is clearly implied thus. For he states, "provided that you read them thus: Written Torah and Oral Torah," and he did not say, "provided that you act concerning them thus." In that chapter, it might have been understood that there was a prohibition against writing Oral Torah matters. But he explicitly states, "provided that you read them thus," meaning: Written Torah from a written text, and Oral Torah not from a written text. However, it is certainly permissible for you to have a hidden text for a time of need. And in this, there was no dispute between Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Yishmael, for certainly all of them speak of the prohibition of learning from a written text, and not of the prohibition of writing.
Rather, according to Rabbi Yochanan, if one violates and renders what is oral into writing, and conversely the reverse, he does not receive reward and will have punishment. But he will not be considered a violator of the covenant, except for one who does not accept the Oral Torah and does not come under its yoke. For truly, if there is no Oral Torah, there is no Written Torah. And this is what Rabbi Yochanan stated in Gittin 60b: "The Holy One, Blessed be He, made a covenant with Israel only on account of the matters of the Oral Torah." That is to say: on account of their observance and belief in them. And as we explained above in section A, this is the truth of the Torah. And similarly, the Rav HaNasi explained above in Section 62 concerning Kilayim, see what is written there, and it will quench your thirst.
And with due apology to the esteemed Rabbi Shelomo Yitzchaki, for we act as emissaries of the Geonim to interpret all the discussions according to their golden measure. And this is the opinion of Rabbi Yochanan, that regarding learning them from a written text, one does not fall under the category of a violator of the covenant. And Rabbi Yishmael disputes him on this, holding that even learning them from a written text is entirely included in the category of a violator of the covenant.
