התמודדות עם ראיות השד"ל: ביאור סוגיות התלמוד — Addressing Shadal's Proofs: Elucidation of Talmudic Passages
שערי תשובה סי' קפ"ז

צילום כתב היד / Scan

נוסח עברי

הגאון שומעין, דאי לאו דברירא ליה מילתא לא היה סומך ראיתו עליהם שר' גופיה מספקא ליה, והוא ברור, וכן בלי ספק היה ענין "אידיהן" או "עידיהן" אי מלשון יום אידם, או מלשון יתנו עידיהן שבריש ע"ז (לח)

English Translation

The Gaon Shim'on is of the opinion that were the matter not clear to him, he would not have relied upon them, since Rabbi himself was in doubt, and this is clear. And likewise, the subject of "Iddeihen" or "Eideihen" was a matter of doubt, whether it derives from the expression "the day of their destruction" (Yom Iddam) or from the expression "they shall give their testimony." And see several other sources by our master, the author of Seder Olam Rabbah, in all of which they appear as "Tanya" (it was taught in a Baraita) or "Tanu Rabbanan" (Our Rabbis taught), yet what is the difference here? But according to what has been stated, the matter, in a similar fashion, of their dispute mentioned in Yoma, remained oral until the time of the later Talmud, and therefore they brought it in the language "It was stated" (Itmar), and this is the truth of the Torah.
And while I was in Vienna, here, in the winter of 5672 [1911-1912], the wise researcher, Rabbi Rachumai, Moreinu Ha-Rav Rabbi Zev ben Rabbi Tuvia, may his light shine, graciously passed into my hands some responsa of the Geonim, and among them a responsum by our master, Rav Sherira Gaon, of blessed memory, which states as follows: "Wherever we say 'Itmar,' from whom does it come, and why do we say 'Itmar' and not 'So-and-so said'?" This matter, that we are accustomed to say 'Itmar,' no Halakhic implication depends on it. And indeed, it is from the exposition at the conclusion that they would recite (up to this point), and they did not attribute that tradition to anyone. Afterwards, it came to them and became known to them from those who stated it, and when it became clarified to them, they taught it in the language 'Itmar' and it was thus established in the Talmud, and not from any known person. From this, the intention of the Gaon is explained: to make known that not because the saying (Ma'amara) was taught in the language 'Itmar' without the name of its speaker, is there any Halakhic consequence, meaning that no Halakhic implication depends on it. He wishes to say that due to this change in language, it is not a matter of Halakhah whether to accept the matter or not to accept it. And the main point is that all those matters established in the Talmud, before they brought that section in the language 'Itmar,' were already established in the Talmud and were taught orally according to their custom until the days of the Rabbanan Savora'ei. And again, they heard this tradition in the Beth Midrash from reliable sources, and established it in the language 'Itmar,' both in the Talmud and in the recitation, and they did not need to mention any specific person, as its testimony had already been strengthened in the Beth Midrash, and the matter was thoroughly clarified. And it is possible that the later Talmud took the liberty not to mention a matter by the name of its speaker, so as not to bring a chain of names from the first generation to the last generation. And even though everything was written and concealed, nevertheless, since they studied the Talmud orally, it involved great difficulty and effort. And concerning this, the Gaon said, 'And not from any known person,' meaning that since the testimony had already been accepted in the Beth Midrash, they did not need to mention the name of the author of the saying, as is known. Concerning this, indeed, we certainly say the blessing, 'Blessed is He who illuminates the eyes of the wise,' for this is precisely what we were discussing, and no word of it fails. For every ancient saying or dispute of the Tanna'im that was not known until recent times, since it is not in a Baraita nor in a Mishnah, for this reason they bring it in the Talmud in one of the formulations to which the later Talmud became accustomed. And this change neither elevates nor diminishes with regard to the Halakhic ruling, as our master the Gaon, of blessed memory, wrote, that due to this change, no Halakhic implication depends on it, and this is clear.
And after this responsum of our master the Gaon reached my hand, I set out to search, a thorough search, perhaps I would find a trace of this among the early and later authorities. And I found that Rabbi Yad Malakhi, in the entry "Itmar," section 5, found difficulty with what the aforementioned Tosafot Yeshanim wrote. And he appropriately marvels, from some sources that filled his hand, that even though they are later Tanna'im, the Talmud brings them in the name "Tanya" or "Tanu Rabbanan" and the like. And he further brought some places of Tannaitic dispute without mention of Mishnah or Baraita, and finally, he brought the words of the Ritbah there, who wrote as follows: "And even though Rabbi Judah and Rabbi Yonatan are Tanna'im, it is stated in the Gemara as 'Itmar' because this dispute was not taught in a Mishnah or in a Baraita, but rather the Amora'im had a tradition that they (the Tanna'im) disputed in this matter, and thus it was stated in the Beth Midrash by the Amora'im." Thus far his words. Behold, my dear reader! Are these not the words of Rabbi Shim'on Gaon in the aforementioned responsum, found in the mouth of the Ritbah, as we know that all these Rishonim were disciples of the Geonim and heard their teachings orally every day without mentioning their names, as we have shown sufficiently throughout this entire composition of ours. And in passing, we have further gained clear proofs that the Mishnayot, the Baraitot, the Toseftot, and the Talmud of each generation were all written and concealed, and from them they would copy them exactly as written and in their precise language, whether elegant, or in a foreign tongue and Aramaic. And every old tradition that remained oral they would establish in the Gemara and in the oral recitation, according to the language of that generation and according to that style. And so on, until the sealing of the Talmud, such that apart from what the Rabbanan Savora'ei and Rav Yehudai Gaon added to it from ancient oral traditions they possessed, nothing further was added. And in my opinion, the matters have been clarified as the sun at midday, and the version of our master Rav Sherira, "Rabbi said, there are, etc." has been strengthened with chains of iron, for many, many similar instances are found in the Talmud. And praise be to the Blessed God, that as a result, sufficient and true reasons concerning the 'wherefore,' 'why,' and 'when' have come into our hands.
And since it has come into our hands, I shall bring what is written by me in the margin of the book *Shevilei Olam*, part "Asia," page 61a, concerning what the author wrote there, as follows: "There is no doubt that from this, the language of the Mishnah 'according to their Idols' (Iddeihen shel Avodah Zarah), and in the Gemara one authority (Tanna) taught 'Iddeihen' and another taught 'Eideihen,' and they differ concerning the pronunciation of the Persian word. And so it is also in the Arabic language. And see in the *Musaf He-Arukh* at the entry 'Hinoma' what he brought in the name of Vav Chet and what he wrote, and it will no longer be astonishing in your eyes." Thus far his words. And concerning this I wrote as follows: "The author of this work, may his end be good, said: It is not possible to say that Rav and Shemuel dispute regarding the pronunciation of a Persian or Arabic word, given that the source is one. I mean to say that the Hebrew word *Mo'ed* (appointed time, festival) is translated in Arabic as *'Id*, and the Persians and Turks, since their origin is from the sons of Japheth, cannot pronounce the guttural sound *'ayin* and instead pronounce it with the sound of *aleph*, which is *Id*. And since all are of one root and one intent, how is it possible that they dispute merely due to a change in pronunciation without there being a difference in intent from one pronunciation to the other, like from 'Ma'abrin' to 'Me'abrin' where the intent changes? And if there is no change in intent, this dispute is vain and insipid, like the froth of dreams. But the main dispute here too is because of a change in intent. And since they knew that the Mishnah speaks plainly in the Holy Tongue, not an Aramaic translation, nor Persian, nor Arabic, therefore one authority (Tanna) taught 'Iddeihen' from the expression 'the day of their destruction' (Yom Iddam) found in the Bible, and another taught 'Eideihen' from the expression 'they shall give their testimony and be justified,' meaning they bear witness to themselves and their abominations. And fundamentally, anyone who believes in a particular faith and performs some action or speaks some word for the affirmation of his faith is called a 'witness' (Ma'id), both in our language and in Arabic. We say, 'Anyone who recites the Shema without Tefillin is as if he bears false witness concerning himself, and by donning Tefillin, he bears true witness.' And likewise, the Ishmaelites bear witness to their faith and say, 'Ashhadu an la ilaha illallah,' etc. And thus, every nation and tongue, when they celebrate their religious festival, are called, through it, 'bearing witness to..."
