הצורך בחיבור המשנה ויתרונותיו — The Necessity and Advantages of Compiling the Mishnah

נוסח עברי

בו באומרו: וכמה דהוה ב"המ קיים" וכו', להשלים אותו בעד חיזוקים אחרים, ועוד בירורים אחרים, שכולהון מילי דראשונים דהוו גרסי הם משניות רבי, ושאפי' המסכתות כבר היו מסודרות מופסקות ועומדות, אלא שהיה ערבוב בלשונות והאופנים והסדרים, ורבי תקן כל זה, והוסיף איזה פירושים, או דברים שנשנו בימיו, וחוץ מזה כבר הכל היה מקדם קדמתה, ולבא אל תכלית הקדוש הזה הוכרח רבי לסתומי מתני' באותו סידור ובאותו לשון שהיה מסדרה ר"מ שהיה קצר וקרוב לשמוע וללמוד וללמד, כי דבריו של ר"מ היו מחוברים ומסודרים בחיבור וסידור יפה מפני שהיה תלמיד ותיק לר"ע אשר נאמר עליו, שלבו היה פתוח כפתחו של אולם, ובכל זה יגיע מרן הגאון אל מחוז חפצו להשביע נפש השואל השוקקה, בתשובה נצחת, ואת כל ונוכחת, ולזה אחר שסיים הגאון להודיע הצורך הגדול במלאכת רבינו הקדוש, כי היכי דלגרסו רבנן בפה אחד ולשון אחד, קשר הדברים יפה בנתינת טעם לאמר: "משום דהנך ראשונים דקמי חרבן הבית לא אצריכו להכי הואיל ותורה שבע"פ הוא, ולא אתמרו טעמייהו בדברים ידועים כגון תורה דכתיבה, אלא ידעי וגמרי טעמא בלבבהון (ה) וכל אחד מגמר להו לתלמידיו כאדם המספר לחברו ומלמדו באיזה לשון שירצה, וכיון דמכנפין בלשכת הגזית ובבתי מדרשות הם מסודרים לאומרם מיד וממשלתא אית להו (יוחסין, וח"מ חסרונות ושיבושים) בלא עקתא ובלא פחדא, ומן שמיא וכו' כדאמרינן (סנהדרין רפ"א) תניא א"ר יוסי בתחי' וכו' וכו', וכולה (ח"מ) ברייתא עד משרבו תלמידי שמאי וכו', ורבנן דבתרייהו דבימי רבן גמליאל (ח"מ. ויוחסין "הלל" וטס"ן) ורבן שמעון בנו (הוא אבי רבינו הקדוש, דבימי רב"ג דיבנה היו המחלוקות אחרים בין ר"א ובין ר"י, א"כ רשב"ג זה הוא אביו של רבי) אכתי קא שקלו וטרו והות פלוגתא דתנאי בהדי הדדי ולא אפשר למגרסינהו בפה אחד ולשון אחד, וביומי רבי בנו של רשב"ג (הנז"ל ודוק) אסתייע מילתיה ותרצינהו וכתבינהו, והוו מילי דמתניתין כמשה מפי הגבורה אמרן וכאות וכמופת דמיין (ו) ולאו מלביה חברינהו וכו', ומנא

English Translation

And inasmuch as the Bait Ha-Mikdash was still extant, etc., to complete it with further reinforcements and additional clarifications, for all these matters of the Rishonim (early authorities) which they studied were the Mishnayot of Rabbi Yehudah Ha-Nasi. And even the tractates were already arranged, divided, and established, but there was a confusion in the phraseology, methods, and sequences. And Rabbi Yehudah Ha-Nasi rectified all this, and added certain interpretations, or matters that were reiterated in his day. Apart from this, everything was already from remotest antiquity.
And to attain this holy objective, Rabbi Yehudah Ha-Nasi was compelled to redact the Mishnah in the same arrangement and concise language that Rabbi Meir had employed, which was readily comprehensible for listening, studying, and teaching. For the pronouncements of Rabbi Meir were coherently assembled and beautifully ordered, because he was a seasoned disciple of Rabbi Akiva, concerning whom it was stated that his heart was as open as the entrance to the Ulam (Sanctuary).
And by all this, Our Master, the Gaon, will attain his desired goal, to satisfy the yearning soul of the inquirer with a decisive and manifest responsum. Therefore, after the Gaon had concluded by elucidating the great necessity for the sacred endeavor of our holy Rabbi (Yehudah Ha-Nasi), in order that the Rabbanan might study with a unified voice and a single tongue, he aptly connected the matters by offering the following rationale:
Because those early authorities who lived before the destruction of the Bait Ha-Mikdash did not require this, inasmuch as it was Oral Torah, and their rationales were not articulated in fixed terms like the Written Torah. Rather, they comprehended and learned the reasoning in their hearts, and each one would transmit it to his disciples as a person recounts to his friend and instructs him in any language he wished. And since they would gather in the Chamber of Hewn Stone and in the study houses, they were prepared to promulgate their teachings immediately, and they possessed authority, free from duress or apprehension. And from Heaven, etc., as it is stated: It was taught: Rabbi Yose ben Taddai said, etc., etc., and the entire Baraita until when the disciples of Shammai multiplied, etc.
...etc., and the entire *baraita* (in the Ch.M. version) until "when the students of Shammai increased," etc., and the Rabbis who came after them.
that in the days of Rabban Gamliel (in the Ch.M. version; and in the *Yuchasin* version: "Hillel," and T.S.N.) and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel his son (he is the father of Rabbeinu HaKadosh, for in the days of Rabban Gamliel of Yavneh, the disputes were different, between Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua; if so, this Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel is the father of Rabbi), they were still debating, and there was a dispute of Tannaim amongst themselves, and it was not possible to study them with one mouth and one tongue.
And in the days of Rabbi, the son of the aforementioned Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel (and be precise), the matter was divinely aided, and he resolved them and wrote them down, and the words of the Mishnah were as if Moses had said them from the mouth of the Almighty, and they are like a sign and a wonder, and he did not compose them from his own heart, etc.
Iyye Ha-Yam: (ה) Here too, it is not the Gaon's will and intention to clarify whether anything was written down by earlier generations or not, for that is not the focus of his attention in this entire responsum. Rather, his intention is to say that since it is the Oral Law, and it is forbidden to learn anything from it from a written text, it follows that, of necessity, each person would write a record of his traditions, or he would teach his students in the language and manner that he wished or that was convenient for him and was clear in its intention.
Iyye Ha-Yam: (ו) In the Ch.M. version: "And in the days of Rabbi, their matter was aided, that the words of the Mishnah are as if said from the mouth of the Almighty, and they are like a sign and a wonder." Here too, the author of this version did his usual thing of omitting any language of writing from the Epistle, but he trampled and mangled several things until he left behind disordered words that would not emerge not only from the mouth of the Gaon, the chief of all speakers, but even from the most stammering-tongued of the early French and German scholars. What is "their matter of the words of the Mishnah"? Truly, there is nothing that causes a greater deafness of the ears than this!
And not only that, but out of his fear that something related to writing might be understood, he omitted "as if Moses had said them from the mouth of the Almighty" and wrote "as if said from the mouth of the Almighty." For if we picture "as Moses from the mouth of the Almighty," we immediately picture the act of writing, just as Moses wrote everything from the mouth of the Almighty. Therefore, he omitted this as well. And he did not remember that when the Gaon completes his statement with these words: "and they are like a sign and a wonder," it perforce leads us to picture something orderly, fine, connected, and written before our eyes, transcending nature, like all the signs and wonders that our ancestors' physical eyes saw.
And just as to this day, the four compositions of the *Yad HaChazakah* that emerged from the quill of the first in rank and last in time, our master Maimonides, of blessed memory, are like a sign and a wonder before our eyes. For when we see the perplexity of the Talmuds, and seeing the intensity thereof, we enter the gates of his compositions with thanksgiving, and with songs we acclaim him for the wondrous and orderly work in a clear and pure tongue that came forth from before the king in royal raiment and a great crown of gold. And as the Raavad, against his will, told in his praise, and swore by the life of his head that he had performed a great work.
And we do not feel this as much with the composition of the Mishnah, because we have not felt the pain of the perplexities and the arrangements, and the stammering tongues and different language that preceded Rabbi, as we feel it from the sages of the generations of the one Talmud that preceded our master Maimonides. And this matter is as clear as the sun at midday.
And after it is verified for the discerning reader who knows a chapter in the wisdom of language and the composition of texts—both from what I, the author of Iyye Ha-Yam, have written until now, and especially from what we will add to prove in the coming notes—that it is as we have said, that in the *Iggeret* of Rav Sherira Gaon, all language of writing, and any language that would lead one to judge so, was omitted in the Ch.M. version, behold, here is a place and a lodging to take one more small step and to say that after it becomes clear to the reader, as we have said, that the authentic version is that of the *Yuchasin*, and all the language of writing brought therein indeed emerged from the quill of the Gaon, of blessed memory.
If so, it follows from this that the writing of mnemonic records of traditions and the like was never forbidden. And the primary prohibition was only to learn matters of the Oral Law from a written text, and private writing was never forbidden. For were it not so, it would have been proper for the Gaon to have been aroused here and to have opened our eyes—after he said, "and he resolved them and wrote them down"—to give a fundamental reason for how the vow was permitted for Rabbi to write down matters of the Oral Law.
