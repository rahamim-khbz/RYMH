On the contrary, from there it is explicitly proven that the secret writing of the entire Oral Torah, in the hands of every Head of the Bet Din and prophet, was not prohibited from the days of Joshua son of Nun onwards, as we have thoroughly clarified his words in what preceded. On the contrary, I am the one who asserted there that "we have not seen in the Talmud when this grave matter of writing the Oral Torah (according to those who prohibit it) or even the Mishnayot was permitted on account of 'a time to act for the Lord'; this is not found in the Talmud, and verbally everyone agrees, but in the Talmud there is no trace of this." See above in the body of the suitable continuation of the words.
And now let this astute scholar, may he live for many years, tell us that Maimonides is the originator of this novelty. And we searched among his words and found that not only was this verse not mentioned in his words there, but on the contrary, his words declare to Jacob that the secret writing was never prohibited in the hands of the heads of the Sanhedrin. Nor did Maimonides introduce any innovation in his Introduction there concerning Rabbenu HaKadosh, of blessed memory, but only what we heard from our Gaon, Rabbenu Sherira, in his truly delightful Iggeret (Epistle), though in a concise and precise manner, which is "the arrangement of the compilation of the Mishnah in one language and with one voice." And they repeated it to the sages in public and all of them wrote it down and settled everywhere so that the Oral Torah should not be forgotten from Israel. His intention is clear, as the Gaon, of blessed memory, explained in his Iggeret, that due to the confusion of languages and arrangements—some concise and some lengthy, some in an imprecise language and some in a clear language—it was causing the Oral Torah to be forgotten from among Israel. And this was not concerning the writing, for it had already existed from ancient times and primeval years according to his words there, and this is clearer than an egg in kutcha.
And anyone who examines the words of Maimonides in his aforementioned Introduction, from the passage "From the days of Moses our teacher until Rabbenu HaKadosh" until the end of the discussion, in order that they might learn it quickly and not forget it, will see with his eyes and understand with his heart that nothing can be derived from the meaning of his words other than these seven principles:
First: that writing existed from ancient times and primeval years. Second: that it was secret, in the hands of the Roshei Avot Bet Din (Heads of the Rabbinical Courts). Third: that study from written text was prohibited, and particularly in public it was a grave prohibition. Fourth: that until Rabbenu HaKadosh, there was no ordered and perfected compilation. Fifth: that the lack of order and perfection in one language and with one voice was an indirect cause of harm (gerama be-nezikin), leading to the Oral Torah being forgotten from among Israel due to the multitude of troubles, the dispersion of exiles, and similar circumstances. Sixth: that Rabbenu HaKadosh innovated nothing but this ordering and arrangement, and no more. Seventh: that Rabbenu HaKadosh neither prohibited nor permitted, but rather rectified. This means he did not prohibit secret writing, which was already permitted, nor did he permit study from written text, which remained prohibited until the time when necessity broke through the fence and made its own way. And what did Rabbenu HaKadosh do? He rectified the language and the order and the appropriate conciseness and the appropriate expansion, and thus a complete, perfected, accepted, good, and beautiful compilation was created, easy to understand and to hear. This is the deed of that great man, in whose merit we live. And this is what is written in Yevamot 64b: "After all, who arranged the Mishnah? Rabbenu." He did not innovate, nor did he write, but rather rectified what already existed. These are the beautiful and sound principles that emerge from the Introduction of Maimonides, the light of our exile, of blessed memory, and all of them were drawn from the wellspring of living waters, which is the Iggeret of the Gaon Rabbenu Sherira, shining sevenfold like the light of the sun.
Here, then, is the explicit view of Maimonides: that Rabbenu did not permit writing, which was already permitted, nor did he permit study from written text, which already remained prohibited, but rather his work was rectification and arrangement, and nothing more. This is contrary to what the scholar Shemuel David Luzzatto, may his light shine, wrote in his name, even without citing a source. Where is the place where Maimonides wrote thus? And I find no excuse for this scholar, may his light shine, except that he was drawn after what HaRav Chiddah, of blessed memory, wrote in his book Ya'ir Ozen, page 25, side 'd' (daled), for truly all his words there are puzzling, as if Rabbenu was the one who innovated writing specifically for the Mishnah, while for the rest of the Oral Torah it remained prohibited, and he brings this matter in the name of Maimonides and Rabbenu Shemuel HaNagid, as if a herald proclaimed it with force.
And from what Rabbenu Shemuel HaNagid wrote at the beginning of his words: "And Rabbenu Yehudah HaNasi wrote it so that it might endure for many days, lest it be forgotten from the hearts of the readers and perish," it appears, and from this it seemed to him that he was the one who initiated the writing. But he did not recall that further on, Rabbenu Shemuel HaNagid wrote concerning those who preceded Rabbenu HaKadosh that "each one copied for himself what he had learned," which is not to be interpreted as "received for himself what he had learned," as is the simple understanding. Furthermore, he used this very language when stating, "And when Rabbenu HaKadosh came to copy, he had to write, etc." It follows, therefore, that the "copying" in the words of Rabbenu Shemuel HaNagid is actual writing, where each one writes for himself what he has learned. And what was written above, that Rabbenu HaKadosh wrote it so that it might endure for many days, means the arrangement and conciseness, as the Gaon Rabbenu Sherira explained at length in his Iggeret, cited above, and this is clear as the sun.
