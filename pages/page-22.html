נוסח עברי

בהו משום (יוחסין. וחופש מטמונים חסר) דהני מובחרין דרבנן הוו דלקטינהו וחברינו (ח) והנהו הוו תלמידי ר"ע" והאריך הגאון עוד בפעם בשבח ר"ע ור"מ תלמידו, הכל כדי להוכיח למה בחר רבי דרכם לתרוצי מתני' כוותיהו, וגם יודיע הגאון מה הוסיף רבי במשנתנו, ומה שהוכרח לשנות בה גם דברי היחידים, והביא ראיה על זה עד אומרו: "ו כ ד חזו כולי עלמא צורתא (יוחסין, וח"מ "שופרא" ואפשר בק"ע כדי לשרש אחר מחשבת הכתיבה) דתרצתא דמתנ', ואמיתת אילין טעמא (ח"מ, ויוחסין "הדברים" והכל אחד, אבל יותר נאה מ"ש ח"מ) ודיוקא דמילא שבקו כולהון הנך תנוי תניני (בין יוחסין וח"מ) דהוו תני, ופשטין אין הלכתא בכל ארץ ישראל והנך (ח"מ, ויוחסין "והויין" טס"ן) שאר הלכתא כולהו אשתביקו כגון ברייתא דמעיינין להו מאן דשמיע להו (ח"מ, ויוחסין ליתא ואינו צריך) וכמאן דמעיין בפירושי או בלישני" רויחי (ח"מ וכ"י וויין ויוחסין "צויחי" טס"ן) אבל סמכא דישראל על אלין הלכתא הוה וקבלינהו ישראל כד חזינון (ט) באמונה וליכא אדם דא פליג בהון" עד כאן היה גאוננו הולך ומתיר כל חד מד' סעיפי הס' הא' כל אחד לעצמו דהיינו, הראה שהכל הוא תורתן של ראשונים מאנשי כנ"הג ומעלה, והראה מפני מה אמרי' סתם משנה ר"מ בטוב טעם ודעת, והראה גם כן למה רוב החכמים המוזכרים הם תלמידי ר"ע, והראה ג"כ שבספקות שנולדו מחדש, בזה הוא שנפל מחלוקת, ופסקינן הלכות כצד הרבים או הנראה אל הסברא, וכיון שהותרו והתבררו כל ד' סעיפי הספק הראשון בפרטן, על כן כגבור יצא הגאון להשיב עכשיו בכללות על עיקר הספק הא', מט' שבקו קמאי רובא לבתראי" שבלשון זה סגר עליו השואל ספקו האדיר, המחולק לד' סעיפיו וע"ז מסיים ואומר: "על' הדין אורחא תרצינהו" רבי לשיתא סדרי ולאו דשבקו קמאי רובא לבתראי, אלא קמאי לא הוו צריכי לחיבורים, ומילי דמגרסן ע"פ אינון וכל חד וחד מרבנן ידעי להו בקבלה, ולא הוו צריכי לחבורינהו ולמכתב בינייהו (אפשר צ"ל דיניהו) עד דחריב ב"המ" וקמו תלמידיהון ולא ידעי כוותהון והוו צריכין לחבורי, דהא ר"א בן הורקנוס וכו', אלא שלא אמר דבר שלא שמע מפי רבו (י) לעולם, ולהכי אצטריך רבי לחבורי ולתרוצי ש"ס משנה בתר דנח עלמא ב' דורות מן ההוא שמדא של חרבן הבית" ואחר שגאוננו השיב לשואל

English Translation

They gathered them and compiled them, because these were the select of the Sages, and they were the disciples of Rabbi Akiva. And the Gaon further elaborated again in praise of Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Meir, his disciple, all in order to demonstrate why Rabbi chose their method to explain the Mishnah according to their views. And the Gaon also makes known what Rabbi added in our Mishnah, and what compelled him to change even the words of individuals within it, and he brought proof for this until he stated: "And when all the world saw the clear form of the Mishnah, and the truth of these reasons and the precision of the words, they abandoned all those other Tanna'im who used to teach. And the Halakhah was spread throughout the Land of Israel, and all other Halakhot, such as Baraitot, were neglected by those who heard them, and by those who delve into commentaries or broad formulations. But the reliance of Israel was on these Halakhot, and Israel accepted them with faith, and there is no one who disputes them."
Up to the phrase "this is where they disagree," our Gaon proceeded to firmly establish each of the four sections of the first doubt, addressing each one individually.
That is to say, he demonstrated that it is all the teaching of the Rishonim, from the Men of the Great Assembly and earlier. He demonstrated, with sound reason and knowledge, why we say that an anonymous Mishnah is attributed to Rabbi Meir. He also demonstrated why most of the Sages mentioned are disciples of Rabbi Akiva. And he demonstrated as well that it was in doubts that arose anew that disagreement occurred, and that we decide the law according to the majority or according to what appears correct to logical reasoning.
And since all four sections of the first doubt were thus resolved and clarified in their particulars, the Gaon therefore went forth like a mighty warrior to now respond in a general manner to the core of the first doubt, namely, "why did the earlier authorities leave the majority of the work for the later ones?"—the very language with which the inquirer had framed his formidable question, which was divided into its four sections.
And regarding this, he concludes and says: "It was in this manner that Rabbi arranged the six orders of the Mishnah."
"And it is not that the earlier authorities left the majority of the work for the later ones, but rather the earlier authorities did not require compilations. The material was studied orally, and each and every one of the Sages knew it by tradition, and they did not need to compile it or write it down among themselves (perhaps the text should read: their laws) until the Temple was destroyed. Then their disciples arose who did not know as well as they, and they required compilations. For behold, Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus, etc., yet he never (10) said anything that he had not heard from the mouth of his teacher. And for this reason, Rabbi needed to compile and arrange the six orders of the Mishnah after the world had rested for two generations from that persecution associated with the destruction of the Temple."
And after our Gaon answered the inquirer regarding—
8) In the Ch"M version, the text reads: "But as for other Beraitot, we are not concerned with them, for the choicest of the Sages, and those upon whom one can rely, are the disciples of (4) Rabbi Akiva." Here too, we could have said that he omitted the terms "we collected" and "we compiled" so that it would not be understood as referring to writing. However, when we examine it carefully, this version appears more correct, as it does not contain the redundancy of "we collected," etc., again. The main point of the Gaon's reasoning revolves around explaining why we are not concerned with other Beraitot, and he provides the reason that the choicest of the Sages, and those upon whom it is proper to rely, are specifically the disciples of Rabbi Akiva. If so, we do not rely upon Beraitot that did not originate from the disciples of Rabbi Akiva. In this manner, the words of the Gaon, of blessed memory, become interconnected, and the sequence of the language appears like a coin struck from the hand of the Gaon, of blessed memory. And with this, the author of this version emerges vindicated from our court.
9) The author of the version did not notice this point and left it in its pristine state. What is the meaning of "we have seen them"? He should have said, "we have heard them." Rather, it is necessary to conclude that there was a form of writing that had spread throughout all of Israel—that is to say, among its Sages, as is known—but they were careful to study it orally, as he will explain later.
10) In the Ch"M version, he omitted and inverted the matters, and this is its text: "But the earlier authorities did not need to compile matters that they studied with one voice, until (?) the Temple was destroyed, and their disciples who were not like them would say things in their names. For behold, Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus, etc., yet he never said anything that he had not heard from his teacher, and Rabbi needed [to do this] for the reason we explained, after the world had settled from that persecution." The discerning reader who plumbs the innermost chambers of each and every matter will see with his own eyes that the author of this version, in order to completely omit the subject of writing and compilation, diverted the topic to something else. Not only is this entirely unrelated to what the Gaon, of blessed memory, is discussing, but furthermore, it is not connected in any way. Firstly, in place of the phrase "and the material was studied orally," which is written in *Yuchasin* as the reason why they did not need to compile—as is the Gaon's method throughout the entire Epistle, where he constantly repeats this decisive reason in order to establish a peg in a firm place—the author of the version changed it and wrote: "they did not need to compile matters that they studied with one voice." This means that they did not require the work of Rabbi, which was to have all the Mishnayot in a single language and a single voice. Now, aside from the fact that this idea is without good reason, unless through an active change and substitution, there is also the fact that, at any rate, according to this version, he left the Gaon's words without reason or logic as to why they did not need to compile matters and study them with one voice. After all, it is a beneficial matter and not detrimental for all to learn in a single language.
