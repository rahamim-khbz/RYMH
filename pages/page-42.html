And whoever has sailed the sea of the Talmud from his youth, where mighty ships voyage, in the commentaries of the Geonim and the rulings of the great compilers, knows and testifies that due to even weaker inferences than these, the earlier and later authorities enter into a thousand more strained logical difficulties than those before us. And with a sharp knife, they would cut through several Mishnayot and Baraitot, and many major bodies of Halakhah and extensive traditions, all in order to clarify matters according to the principles of sound reasoning and clear truth. As our father, our teacher, and the light of our exile, Rabbi Hezekiah himself, of blessed memory, wrote at the beginning of the Responsum on the Shofar Blasts in Sefer Tamim De'im, responsum number 119, brought below at length and in full at the end of section 353 in my annotations, and he wrote as follows: "And the words of the many are the foundational proof for every Mishnah and for every Gemara, and the rest, to my Master and Teacher, of blessed memory." And it is a religious obligation to bring from the new what I found by Rabbeinu ha-Me'iri in his introduction to Tractate Avot, published in Salonica with Sefer Darkhei Chayyim by the distinguished Rabbi Meir Schnei, may his light shine. And we shall copy the words of Rabbeinu ha-Me'iri, of blessed memory, passages that are necessary for our consideration, and he wrote as follows: "Know that throughout all the periods mentioned, until the end of the prophetic era, which is the time of the destruction of the First Temple, they did not see themselves as needing to write down anything that was meant to be transmitted orally. They had nothing written save the Torah and the Prophets, for all that was necessary for them, from the Talmudic explanations of the commandments, was transmitted to them completely, person to person. And concerning what arose anew and required deliberation according to the novelty of the circumstances, they were transmitted the fundamental principles and hermeneutic rules, from which they would bring their hidden matters to light and clarify all the laws. And what inference and reasoning could not sufficiently address, they would sometimes follow the majority, as it is said: 'after the many, to incline.' And sometimes prophecy would clarify every hidden matter for them, as you know from the words of some of our perfect Sages, that prophecy declares intellectual insights which study cannot attain, much less mere reasoning. For just as the imaginative faculty can attain understanding that the senses cannot reach, so too the intellectual faculty can attain understanding that it cannot reach through study, premises, or reasoning. Nevertheless, they would deliberate and engage in discussion through the ways of inference, hermeneutic rules, and reasoning to bring their hidden matters to light. And all this without any dispute among them at all, for they were then devoted to the prophets, subject to their authority, and hearkened to them. And sometimes they would rely on matters whose content was traditionally received by them as Halakhah le-Moshe mi-Sinai, and this was in a matter over which neither study nor reasoning, nor any of the hermeneutic rules by which the Torah is expounded, held sway. And sometimes they would also base their traditions on scriptural verses merely as an allusion. And everything remained for them by way of tradition, from person to person, along with what their leaders would innovate for them according to the time and according to the newly arising need, without dispute, etc., etc." But when the time of the Men of the Great Assembly arrived, they recognized the beginning of their deficiency, and each one wrote for himself what he had heard from his master, whether the first of the Sages from what they had heard from the last of the Prophets, after they sensed the absence of prophecy and the beginning of dispute with those who came after them concerning what they had heard from person to person. And this is what they said in the first chapter of Yevamot, folio 16a: Rabbi Dosa ben Horkynas said, "Upon this very seat sat Haggai the Prophet and declared three matters: first, the rival wife of a daughter is permitted to the brothers, etc., and another, etc., and another, etc." And this account was not known except through it being found written. For Rabbi Dosa was in the time of Rabbi Akiva, and from the time of Rabbi Akiva to the time of Haggai is close to five hundred years. And although we said there that Rabbi Dosa ben Horkynas, who was an exceedingly aged man, said "I call heaven and earth to witness that upon this seat, etc.," which implies that these words were spoken in his presence and he was the one testifying, it is possible that he found it written thus, and testified from some writing of some expert, for there is no meticulousness required for a testimony concerning a Halakhic ruling in this matter whatsoever. etc., etc. And let us return to our matter, which is that in the time of the Men of the Great Assembly, the Rashbatz wrote in one place that some of the prophets lived long until the days of the Tannaim, as mentioned in Yevamot: "Rabbi Dosa ben Horkynas, etc." See there at length. And I found written in the margin of the book, in my own handwriting and signature, and this is my wording: "See the Me'iri in his introduction to Avot, who on the contrary, argued from this that this tradition from the prophets was written down by Rabbi Dosa ben Horkynas, contending with a natural argument: how could there be a gap of five hundred years? And if one of the early Rabbis of France and Ashkenaz, whose holy nature it was to tolerate several exaggerations, had written this matter, it would be acceptable. But a Gaon in his generation like the Rashbatz, a scholar and critic, a healer and physician, and mighty as a lion, how could he tolerate writing such a thing? It is indeed astounding. Thus far the words of the author, may his end be good." And incidentally to this annotation of mine, I remembered and took into my hands the Me'iri we are discussing.
