ואם ראה הכרח לפרש המשניות א"כ למה יניח ר' דברים סתומים? היה לו לבאר המשניות היטב ובזה יכלול כל מלאכת ר"ח ולזה אוחז הספק ואומר: "ודאמריתו מה ראה ר' חייא לכותבם? ואמאי לא כתבם רבי? ולזה משיב: אלו בקש רבי לכתוב (יא) ולחבר כל מה שהיה שנוי בימיו אריכן מילי ואתיקרן אלא ר' עקרי הדברים תקן. וכתב וכגון כללות (ח"מ, ויוחסין "וכגוון קלות") ולישנא קיטא, דאפי' מחדא מילתא מגמר ממנה כמה טעמי" וכו' וכו' ואתא ר' חייא ופשט בברייתא פרטי" וכו' והאריך הגאון ז"ל קצת הדיבור בזה, להורות הפינה האמיתית והיא: שבמתני' כלל הכל, ושהעיקר לענין הלכה היא מתני' ולא בריית' דאי רבי לא שנא ר' חייא מנין ליה, ולזה השלים דבריו באומרו: "ודוכי דטעמי דנפישין ומרווחין בברייתא עיקר דלהון

English Translation

Thus he said: "And regarding the tractates which you have seen to contain both earlier and later material, you have arranged them for the Mishnah; you have not arranged them as one tractate with later and earlier material, for they are like two separate fields, etc., etc. But the Halakhah and the chapters of each and every tractate, you have arranged them one after another. For Rav Huna said: In one tractate, we do not say 'there is no order to the Mishnah,' but in two tractates, we do say 'there is no order to the Mishnah.' And regarding each one of them, it can be said that Rabbi Judah the Prince taught it first. And wherever we find an anonymous statement followed by a dispute concerning legal matters in a single tractate, we say: The Halakhah is not in accordance with the anonymous statement, etc., etc. Nevertheless, we say concerning the tractates of Neziqin that all of them constitute a single tractate."
And immediately thereafter, the Gaon, of blessed memory, proceeded to answer the aforementioned question in all its four sections, one by one, and on the very last point. And here, to begin with, he strengthened the questioner's opinion that Rabbi Ḥiyya is certainly the master of Baraitot, and that the time he compiled them cannot be clarified, inasmuch as the Talmud is in doubt as to whether Rabbi Ḥiyya passed away before Rabbi Judah the Prince or after him. If so, then the time of compilation of the Baraitot is also necessarily subject to this doubt, which is like a balanced equation. Rather, this matter can be clarified from another source, for in the days of Rabbi Judah the Prince, the Toseftot and Baraitot were arranged. And he brings proofs for this, and thus he said: "Regarding Ḥiyya, it is he who arranged it, and the matter of the Tosefta is not concluded for us. Certainly, if in the days of Rabbi Judah the Prince, etc., as we wonder about the subject, etc. It is therefore a doubt for us. Nevertheless, the Rabbis said that in the days of Rabbi Judah the Prince, they were arranged, etc., and it appears from that saying in Tractate Ḥagigah that they knew Halakhah, Sifra, Sifrei, Tosefta, and the entire Talmud." And this is a complete proof that in the days of Rabbi Judah the Prince, the Toseftot already existed.
And after he clarified the first section of the aforementioned question, he immediately proceeded to the second section, which is: If so, that in the days of Rabbi Judah the Prince the Baraitot and Toseftot were compiled, what necessity did Rabbi Ḥiyya see for this work? And if he saw a necessity to explain the Mishnah, then why would Rabbi Judah the Prince leave matters anonymous? He should have explained the Mishnah thoroughly, and in this way, all of Rabbi Ḥiyya's work would have been encompassed. Therefore, the doubt arises, and he says: "And you said, what did Rabbi Ḥiyya see to write them? And why did Rabbi Judah the Prince not write them?" To this, he replied: If Rabbi Judah the Prince had sought to write and compile all that was taught in his days, the matters would have become lengthy and costly. Rather, Rabbi Judah the Prince established the main points and wrote in a general manner and concise language, such that even from one statement, many reasons can be derived, etc., etc. And Rabbi Ḥiyya came and explained the details in the Baraita, etc.
"and the details are explained in the Baraita," etc. And the Gaon, of blessed memory, elaborated somewhat on this matter, to teach the true cornerstone, which is: that in the Mishnah, he included everything, and that the primary source for halakhic ruling is the Mishnah and not the Baraita—for if Rebbi did not teach it, from where would Rabbi Chiya know it? And to this end, he completed his words by saying: "And the places where the reasons are numerous and expansive as in the Baraita, their essence is in the Mishnah."
In the version of Ch"M, page 24, it is written: "If Rebbi had sought to say, concerning all that he taught, long and weighty words... rather, Rebbi established the essential points of the matters, such as general principles and concise language." Here too, it is visible to every eye how he substituted and omitted according to his will and left matters unfit. For behold, at the outset, there is no one who can deny the astonishing thing that is apparent according to the version of the author of the *Nusach*.
Since the questioner consistently uses the language of "writing" regarding R' Chiya and regarding Rebbi, how is it that no sharp word came from the mouth of the Gaon, of blessed memory, to awaken the slumbering and say: "And regarding that which you said, 'What did R' Chiya see to write them, and why did Rebbi not write them?'—R' Chiya did not *write* them, but rather he formulated them or arranged them. And if Rebbi had sought to arrange, formulate, and compose, etc."? And from the fact that he was not aroused to make this point, this, at any rate, constitutes a small but significant proof for the version of the *Yuchasin*, without a doubt.
Furthermore, who cannot see that the B.K.'A. removed the two words "to write and to compose" and put in their place the word "to say," which is not at all relevant, as is understood. What is the meaning of "to say"? He should have said "to establish," as he concludes, "he established the essential points of the matters." And furthermore, what is the meaning of "to say, concerning all that he taught"? It has no meaning at all, and God forbid that these convoluted and incomprehensible words should have come from the mouth of our Gaon, of blessed memory. What author would use the language of "saying" in place of "arranging" or "formulating" or "composing"?
By my life! It was a great miracle for us that the author of the *Nusach* did not know his right from his left, and through this, he illuminated our eyes to find the errors and to clarify the one true version.
