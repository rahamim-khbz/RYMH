הענף הב': ביאור אגרת רב שרירא גאון — Branch Two: Elucidation of Rav Sherira Gaon's Epistle
שערי תשובה סי' קפ"ז

צילום כתב היד / Scan

נוסח עברי

לתלמידי דילנא, והאי כתיב באנפא חדא, והאי כתוב באנפא אחרינא הכי הוו מפרשין משנתהון וקרו להו לפירושיהון תלמוד" כונת הגאון מבוארת בזה להשמיענו דקודם שכתב רבי חיבור המשנה, היו גם כן שני אופני לימודים, האחד: דינים פסוקים כמו שהוא המשנה, והב': הפלפול והפי' והטעמים כמו שהוא התלמוד, וכל שני מיני לימודים הללו, לא היה לכל חד בדידיה לשון אחד ופה אחד לכל רבנן, כי אם כל אחד היה שונה הדינים הפסוקים, שהם המשנה, וטעמי הדינים, והמשא ומתן שהוא הנקרא תלמוד, במין לשון משונה מחברו, ובמין וסדר ואופן בפני עצמו, לפי ערך המלמדים והתלמידים, כמו שהוא גם כן.
בימינו, ועד ימות עולם, ואחרי חיבור המשנה הרויחו אותה המבוכה שהיתה בלימודה, והיו כולם שונים בפה אחד ולשון אחד, אבל עדין בימי רבי עצמו היו מפרשין משנתהון (שלימוד על אופן זה הוא הנקרא תלמוד) כל אחד בסדר ואופן משונה כמו שהיה מקודם דעדיין לא הוצרכו לתקן גם דבר זה כיון שהיה לבם פתוח כפתחו של אולם, ולזה מסיים דבריו "והוה רויחא לבא ולא הוו צריכים (ר"ל ללימוד התלמוד שהוא הפלפול) אלא עיקרים (ולא אורך דברים) וכד אסתיים משנה, ונח נפשיה דרבי, אמעט לבם ואצריכו למלקט תלמודיהו ולמגריסיה (טז) (אלא שכדי להבין הדברים בשורשן הוכרחו להוסיף בתלמוד ארחי אחריני על אותם של הראשונים וז"א:) ואוסיפו ביה כמה ארחתא אחרנייתא (על) דתקינו ראשוני ראשונים (לדוגמא) כדאמרינן מנאה"מ, ודרשינן קראי (שהוא מעין מלאכת סוס"פ בתוספת חיקור וביאור) ותקינו ביה חידושי, ותולדות, מלתא מלתא, כד מפרשינן כי היכי דהוה בימי קדמונים". ר"ל שהתקינו חידושי דינים דהיינו הבעיות, ופושטין אותם ממשנה או ברייתא, ומזה יצאו תולדות לאלפים בכל דין ודין. ולא שהיו חדשים ממש, אלא שהיו נכללים בקיצור לשון המשנה והברייתא, ובסידור הלכותיהם, ועל ידי הפלפול והבעיות חזרו מפורשים ומבוררים כי היכי שהיו ידועים ומובנים לראשונים, מבלי כל הטרחא הזאת. והולך ומוסיף הגאון שלא זו בלבד החידושים בדרך תולדות הוא שהוסיפו ביה בתלמודם אלא גם ביאור דברים עמוקים שלא היו מובנים לאחרונים שלפי שללבן של ראשונים היה כל זה ללא צורך שכבר היו חדים ומבינים כל דבר בקיצור מילין וזהו אומרו: "ותקינו ביה (בכל תלמוד שמתחדש בכל דור ודור) מאי דלא הוו צריכין ביה הנך קמאי, כגון פי' דברים עמוקים, דהנך קמאי שהיה לבן רחב, לא היו צריכין לפרשן (ועכשיו שבירר הגאון שמיעוט הלב הזה כבר נולד גם קודם רבי וכ"ש בימי רבי וז"א:) ונתמעטה אחרי הם

English Translation

To our disciples: "And this is formulated in one aspect, and this is articulated in another aspect. Thus would they expound their Mishnah, and they called their expositions 'Talmud'." The intention of the Gaon is explained herein, to inform us that even before Rabbi Judah the Prince compiled the Mishnah, there were likewise two modes of study: one consisted of explicit legal rulings (Halakhot Pesukot), such as the Mishnah; and the second, the dialectical reasoning (Pilpul), the explanation, and the reasons, such as the Talmud. For neither of these two types of study was there a single language and a single expression for all the Rabbis; rather, each one would teach the explicit legal rulings, which constitute the Mishnah, and the reasons for the rulings, and the give-and-take (Massa u-Matan) which is called Talmud, in a form of language differing from his colleague's, and in a unique order and manner, according to the capacity of the teachers and the students, just as it is in our days, and will be until the end of days.
And after the compilation of the Mishnah, they were relieved of the confusion that had existed in its study, and all would teach with a single expression and a single language. But still, in the days of Rabbi Judah the Prince himself, they would expound their Mishnah, each one in a different order and manner, as it had been before, for they had not yet needed to rectify this matter, since their hearts were as open as the entrance to the Sanctuary. And for this reason, he concludes his remarks: "And their hearts were expansive, and they did not require detailed explanations, but rather main principles. And when the Mishnah was completed, and Rabbi Judah the Prince passed away, their hearts diminished, and they found it necessary to gather their Talmud and to study it diligently."
And they added to it several other paths that the earliest predecessors had established, as we say, "From where do we know it?" and we expound verses. And they established in it novel interpretations (Hiddushim) and derivations (Toldot), item by item, as we explain, as it was in the days of the ancients. That is to say, they established novel legal rulings, which are the questions, and they resolve them from the Mishnah or Baraita. And from this, thousands of derivations emerged for each and every law. Not that they were entirely new, but rather they were implicitly included in the concise language of the Mishnah and the Baraita, and in the arrangement of their laws. And through the dialectical reasoning and the questions, they became explicit and clarified, just as they had been known and understood by the early authorities, without all this exertion.
And the Gaon proceeds to add that not only were innovations in the form of derivations added to their Talmud, but also explanations of profound matters which were not understood by later generations, since for the early authorities, all this was unnecessary, as they were already keen and understood everything with concise words. And this is what he states: "And they established in it what those early ones did not need, such as explanations of profound matters, for those early ones, whose hearts were expansive, did not need to explain them."
And their hearts diminished after them. Indeed, one is culpable by the judgment of the court of justice, for he subtracts even without knowledge, without deliberation, and without wisdom, and he left behind matters worn out and patched up, and fell into his own flabbiness. Who would not plainly see the deficiency he created by curtailing a "written compilation" with brevity, and left matters truly on the surface of the waters? For he states, "and those early ones did not have," without saying what it was that they did not have. And let the esteemed critic not err by interpreting it thus: "and they did not have any organized matter, etc.," but rather, for example, the explanations we give to students, for behold, he states immediately thereafter, "that we teach it with a single expression." This extracts the stolen item from the hand of the copyist, for to what does the word "it" refer, since no specific item was mentioned beforehand, and upon what does it rest? Furthermore, who is wise enough to understand this? For in truth, there is no sense or understanding to these words as they are written: "and they did not have," etc., "until Rabbi... that we teach it...." And far be it from the Gaon, the foremost speaker everywhere, that he should write such unconnected and unintelligible words, unless he alluded to and snatched away two good distinctions, "a written compilation," and did not know what to put in their place, because he did not know what the Rabbis were saying. Therefore, he left the words without a soul and departed. And this is besides the fact that the flow of the entire passage proves it, for he brings a parable similar to the matter being expounded, just as the later authorities have written commentaries in different ways, and all lead to one understanding; so it was for the early authorities. If so, just as these are in writing, so too were the early authorities in writing. The difference is only that the early authorities studied them orally, while the later authorities were permitted to study them from writing. And this is the truth for everyone who acknowledges the truth. And I will not conceal that in the manuscript (Ketav Yad) which is in the library of Wien, may its glory endure, much of which is in the version of the author of Ha-Ofesh. Also in this, it matches letter for letter precisely with the version of Ha-Ofesh which is before us. But the truth is not absent, even if many witnesses contradict it, when they have refuting evidence from sound reason, from the ways of acknowledging truth, and from the rules of language and the order of matters.
Behold, reader, another proof, from his statement "to gather their Talmud," which is a compilation of written material, so that they could arrange it orally, as he immediately concludes, "and to study it diligently." And note throughout the words of this epistle that whenever the Gaon mentions the matter of writing, or compilation, or gathering, he is immediately careful to explain his words, that everything was in oral recitation, not from a written text.
